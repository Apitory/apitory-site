Pay-Per-Result Pricing, Explained: Why Data Should Be Charged by the Delivered Record

Most data is sold by subscription, by seat, or by the raw row — three models that charge you for the vendor's inventory rather than your outcome. Pay-per-result charges only for records actually delivered and usable. This article explains how it works, where it is fair, and how to compare it honestly against the alternatives.

The problem with how data is usually priced

Every pricing model quietly decides who carries the risk of failure. In data products, failure has specific shapes: the record with no contact details, the job listing for a role that closed last month, the company record that turns out to be a duplicate. The question is never whether failures exist — in any real dataset they do — but whose bill they land on.

Subscriptions put all the risk on the buyer. You pay for access to an inventory for a period, whether you use it heavily, lightly or not at all, and whether the slice you need is fresh or stale. The vendor's revenue is secured the day you sign; the incentive to keep your corner of the database current is whatever their churn anxiety provides.

Per-seat pricing prices your team, not the data. It made sense for software whose cost scaled with users; for data delivery it is an arbitrary multiplier that punishes you for letting more colleagues touch the same records.

Per-row pricing looks outcome-based and is not. A row is a unit of extraction, not a unit of value: the row for a business with no published contact, the row for a closed job, and the row you can actually use are all charged identically. Per-row vendors are paid for attempts. The buyer absorbs the yield.

Pay-per-result starts from the other end: define the result — the thing the buyer is actually trying to obtain — and charge when, and only when, it is delivered.

What "result" means, precisely

The model only works if the result is defined sharply enough to be checked by both sides. In our own Actors the definitions are deliberately mechanical:

  • Google Maps leads: a place record costs $0.004 — places are cheap to extract and the buyer wants the full enumeration. The lead charge of $0.10 applies only when the record carries a usable public contact. A place that yields no contact never attracts the premium. (See the complete guide for how the two steps work.)
  • Job data: $3.00 per 1,000 job records delivered from live ATS feeds. A company on your list with no open roles delivers no records and costs nothing beyond the run's start event — and because the pull is live, every delivered record is a role the employer itself currently lists as open.
  • UK company data: $0.001 per full record — company profile plus officers and persons with significant control — fetched from the official register at run time. You pay for the companies you asked about, assembled complete.
  • Telegram channels: $0.0015 per post delivered to your dataset. A monitoring run that finds twelve new posts costs twelve posts' worth, not a month of subscription.

Two properties are doing the work in each definition. The unit is the buyer's unit of value, not the vendor's unit of effort. And the event is verifiable by the buyer: you can count the records in your dataset, check which ones carry contacts, and reconcile the bill against the delivery line by line. A pricing model you can audit is a pricing model that disciplines its vendor.

The incentive argument — why this is not just a discount shape

Pricing models are incentive structures wearing invoices. Under per-row pricing, a vendor earns more by extracting more rows, so the product drifts toward volume: wider nets, looser filters, duplicates tolerated. Under pay-per-result, the vendor earns more only when the buyer's yield rises, so the product drifts toward the unglamorous work that actually moves yield — unwrapping redirect-hidden website URLs before fetching them (a real bug we found and fixed in our own Maps engine, where it had been silently suppressing email yield), filtering out parked domains, deduplicating properly, keeping parsers current when sources change.

The same logic applies to freshness. A subscription database is paid the same whether its records are a day or a year old. A per-result vendor selling live pulls is paid per delivery, and the delivery is only worth buying if it is current — the model's revenue and the data's freshness point in the same direction. When you evaluate any data product, it is worth asking the vendor a simple question: "What do you earn more of when my results get worse?" If the answer is "the same" or "more", you have learned something important.

Comparing honestly: when pay-per-result is NOT the cheapest option

This model is not universally optimal, and a vendor who claims otherwise is selling. Three situations favour the alternatives:

  • Very high, steady volume. If you consume millions of records a month on a stable specification, a negotiated flat arrangement will usually beat any per-unit price — ours included. Per-result pricing wins on alignment and zero commitment, not on infinite scale economics.
  • Exploratory access. If you need to browse a broad inventory to discover what exists, an access subscription can be the right tool. Per-result is built for defined questions: these companies, this territory, this channel.
  • Data as a by-product. Where records are a cheap side output of a platform you already pay for, marginal pricing comparisons stop meaning much.

There is also a cost the buyer still carries under our model, and it should be named: Apify runs consume platform usage (compute and proxies) in addition to the Actor's per-result charges, under Apify's own pricing. The per-result charge is the price of the data work; the platform it runs on bills separately, the same as a database subscription sits on top of your cloud bill. We consider the two-line bill a feature — each line is auditable — but it is a line you should know exists before your first run, not after.

How to audit a pay-per-result bill (a five-minute checklist)

  1. Count the delivered records in the dataset and multiply by the unit price. It should match the charge exactly; if the pricing has tiers or minimums, they should be stated on the Actor's page before you run.
  2. Spot-check the premium events. If you were charged lead premiums, sample twenty premium records and confirm each carries the contact the premium is defined on. This is the audit per-row vendors cannot offer you, because in their model there is nothing to check.
  3. Check the failures. Sample the records you were not premium-charged for. They should be genuinely contact-free — a business with no published email — not extraction misses. A vendor whose free rows are full of findable contacts is under-delivering, whatever the bill says.
  4. Verify freshness on a known record. Pick one entity whose current state you know — a company whose director changed, a job that closed yesterday — and confirm the delivered record matches reality. Verifiability before commitment is the standard we recommend for all data buying, including ours; our Companies House guide describes the same three-company check for register data.

If a vendor passes those four checks, the pricing model is doing its job. If a vendor cannot be checked this way, the pricing model is decoration.

A worked comparison: the same campaign under three pricing models

Models are easiest to judge on one concrete job, so take the campaign from our lead generation guide: a territory extraction returning 1,000 places, of which — after filtering — 700 are enriched and 400 yield a usable public contact. Under per-row pricing at an illustrative $0.05 per extracted record, you pay for all 1,000 rows: $50, including the 600 rows that never become leads. Under a subscription database, the campaign's cost is whatever fraction of the monthly fee you assign to it — but the records come from the vendor's last compilation, not from this week's listings, and the 400-contact subset is whatever their coverage happens to hold. Under pay-per-result at our prices, the bill is $4 for the places plus $40 for the 400 records that carry a usable contact: $44, with every premium dollar attached to a record you can open and inspect. The interesting comparison is not the totals — they are deliberately close. It is what each bill is for. The per-row bill is for attempts. The subscription bill is for access. The per-result bill is for the 400 outcomes, and it can be audited record by record against them. When the campaign repeats next quarter in a new territory, only the third bill scales with what the territory actually yields.

If you negotiate data contracts: definitions to nail down

Per-result pricing moves the negotiation from price to definitions, which is where it always belonged. Whatever vendor you are dealing with — us included — get these five definitions in writing before volume starts:

  • The result. Exactly what a billable record must contain. "A lead" is not a definition; "a place record carrying at least one publicly published email address or a contact-page URL" is.
  • The duplicate rule. Are duplicates billed? Ours are a data-quality failure, not a product: deduplication on identity (name plus address, company number, job ID) happens before delivery, and a duplicate that slips through is not a result.
  • The freshness rule. When was the record produced? For live-pull products the answer is "during your run"; for anything else, the compilation date should be stated per record, not per sales page.
  • The failure bill. What does a run that finds nothing cost? Under our model, a minimal start event and nothing else. A vendor whose empty runs are expensive is charging for attempts under a result-shaped label.
  • The audit right. Can you sample delivered records against the source and dispute charges that fail the definition? A vendor confident in its pipeline will say yes in writing, because the audit is the mechanism that keeps both sides honest between renewals.

Notice that none of these definitions mention price. That is the point: in data buying, most disappointment is definition-shaped, not price-shaped. A cheap record that is not what you meant to buy is expensive; a fairly priced record that exactly matches a written definition compounds in value every time you reuse the pipeline.

The deeper pattern: software is moving to outcome pricing everywhere

Data is not the only category repricing this way. Across software, the unit of charge has been migrating from access (seats, licences, subscriptions) toward outcomes (resolved tickets, delivered records, completed tasks) as the marginal cost of the work itself becomes measurable per unit. The driver is the same in every category: when a vendor can measure exactly what it delivered, it can charge for exactly that — and buyers, once they have seen a bill they can audit line by line against outcomes, rarely accept an access bill again. Data products feel the shift first because their outcomes are unusually countable: a record either carries the contact or it does not; a job is either open or it is not; a company record is either complete or it is not. We built Apitory's pricing on the assumption that this migration is permanent, and that the vendors who thrive under it will be the ones whose pipelines genuinely produce the outcomes — because under per-result pricing, there is no longer anywhere for a decaying product to hide its decay. If you buy data regularly, that is the future to negotiate toward, with whichever vendor can survive being audited in it.

Who pays when the source breaks?

Every data pipeline breaks eventually, because every source changes eventually: a site restructures, a platform revises its feed, a register updates its interface. The interesting question is whose problem that is under each pricing model — because the answer predicts how fast it gets fixed. Under a subscription, the vendor is paid in full while the product degrades, and the buyer's remedy is a complaint and a churn threat at renewal; the repair queue competes with every other priority in the vendor's roadmap, and the buyer cannot even see the degradation without auditing records by hand. Under per-row pricing, breakage is worse than invisible — it can be profitable, if broken extraction produces billable rows of garbage. Under per-result pricing with a sharp result definition, breakage stops the vendor's revenue for the affected product: records that fail the definition do not bill, empty runs do not bill, and the vendor's income resumes exactly when the pipeline is repaired. That alignment does not make breakage impossible; it makes the vendor's incentive point at the fix with an urgency no SLA clause manufactures. When you evaluate a vendor, ask about the last time a source broke and what happened to billing during the outage. The answer tells you which model you are really buying, whatever the pricing page calls it.

Forecasting your spend: the small-run method

The standard objection to usage pricing is unpredictability — a subscription is a known number, a per-result bill is not. In practice the bill is forecastable with one cheap experiment, because yields are properties of the market, not of the run. Run the smallest version of your real job: one territory instead of ten, fifty companies instead of five hundred, one channel's backfill tranche instead of the whole set. Measure the delivered results and the charge. Your full job's cost is that measurement scaled by the size ratio, with a margin for the variance between territories — and the small run was not a cost of forecasting, it was the first tranche of the work itself, fully usable. This is how our own buyers typically start: a smoke-scale run whose output they audit (the audit checklist above), followed by volume once the yield and the quality are measured facts rather than sales claims. A vendor confident in its pipeline will encourage exactly this sequence. A vendor who insists on an annual commitment before you may measure anything is telling you how the measurement would go.

FAQ

Is pay-per-result the same as pay-per-click or usage-based pricing?

It is a form of usage-based pricing with a stricter definition of usage: not attempts, API calls or rows processed, but results delivered that meet the stated definition — a record with a usable contact, a live job, a complete company record, a collected post.

What do I pay when a run finds nothing?

For most of our Actors, little or nothing beyond a minimal start event ($0.00005). If a Maps territory has no matching businesses or a company list has no open roles, there are no results to charge for. That is the model working as intended.

Are there minimum charges or subscriptions?

No subscriptions and no monthly minimums on our Actors. You pay the per-result charges for what a run delivers, plus Apify's platform usage for the run itself under Apify's pricing.

How do I verify I was charged correctly?

Count records in the delivered dataset, sample the premium-charged records to confirm each meets the premium's definition, and reconcile against the run's charge breakdown in the Apify console. The five-minute checklist above walks through it.

When should I choose a subscription instead?

When your volume is very high and steady, when you need open-ended browsing of a broad inventory, or when the data is a by-product of a platform you already pay for. The article above gives the honest cases; per-result wins on alignment, auditability and zero commitment.

Does per-result pricing mean the data is guaranteed accurate?

No pricing model can guarantee that. What it guarantees is the direction of the vendor's incentive and your ability to audit the delivery. Accuracy itself comes from the source — live pulls from the systems of record — which is why our Actors fetch at run time rather than reselling stored data.
