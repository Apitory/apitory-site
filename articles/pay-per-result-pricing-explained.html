Pay-Per-Result Pricing for Data Products: How Outcome Billing Works

TL;DR: Pay-per-result bills only for delivered, verified records rather than raw extraction attempts or subscriptions. Forecast spend with a small pilot run and audit every charge against your output dataset.

Start With One Concrete Campaign: Three Pricing Models Side by Side

Most data is still sold by subscription, by seat, or by the raw row—three models designed around a vendor's inventory rather than your outcome. Every pricing model quietly decides who carries the risk when something goes wrong: a business record with no contact details, a job listing for a role that closed last month, or a company entry that turns out to be a duplicate. Failure exists in every real dataset. The real question is whose bill it lands on.

The clearest way to judge pricing models is on a single concrete job, so take the campaign from our Google Maps lead generation guide: a territory extraction that returns 1,000 places, of which—after filtering—700 are enriched and 400 yield a usable public contact.

  • Per-row pricing: At an illustrative $0.05 per extracted record, you pay for all 1,000 rows—$50—including the 600 rows that never become leads. A row is a unit of extraction, not a unit of value. The row for a business with no published contact, the row for a closed job, and the row you can actually use are priced identically. Per-row vendors are paid for attempts, while the buyer absorbs the yield.
  • Subscription database pricing: Your campaign cost is whatever fraction of the monthly fee you assign to it, but the records come from the vendor's last compilation rather than this week's listings, and the 400-contact subset is limited to whatever their existing coverage happens to hold. Subscriptions place all the risk on the buyer: you pay for access for a period whether you run heavily, lightly, or not at all, and whether your slice is fresh or stale. The vendor's revenue is locked in the day you sign, leaving only churn anxiety to keep your corner of the database current.
  • Per-seat pricing: Seat licences price your team instead of the data. That made sense for software whose cost scaled with users, but for data delivery it acts as an arbitrary multiplier that penalises you for letting more colleagues touch the same records.
  • Pay-per-result pricing: At our prices, the bill for that territory is $4 for the places plus $40 for the 400 records carrying a usable contact: $44 total, with every premium dollar tied to a record you can open and inspect.

Notice that the interesting comparison is not the headline totals—they are deliberately close. What matters is what each invoice pays for. The per-row bill pays for attempts. The subscription bill pays for access. The per-result bill pays for the 400 outcomes, auditable record by record against the delivered file. When the campaign repeats next quarter in a new territory, only the pay-per-result bill scales with what that territory actually yields.

The Broader Shift From Access to Measurable Outcomes

Data is not the only category repricing around outcomes. Across software, the unit of charge has been migrating from access—seats, licences, and subscriptions—toward outcomes such as resolved tickets, delivered records, and completed tasks as the marginal cost of each unit of work becomes measurable. Whenever a vendor can measure exactly what it delivered, it can bill for exactly that, and buyers who have reconciled an outcome-based invoice rarely go back to paying blindly for access.

Data products feel this shift first because their deliverables are unusually countable: a record either carries a contact or it does not, a job is either open or it is not, and a company filing is either complete or it is not. We built Apitory's pricing on the premise that this migration is permanent—and that under pay-per-result billing, a decaying pipeline no longer has anywhere to hide.

What Counts as a Delivered Result (and What It Looks Like)

Pay-per-result starts from the buyer's end: define the exact outcome you are trying to obtain, and charge when—and only when—it is delivered. The model only holds together if "result" is defined mechanically enough for both sides to verify.

  • Google Maps leads: A place record costs $0.004, because places are inexpensive to extract and buyers often want the full territory enumeration. The $0.10 lead charge applies only when the record carries a usable public contact; a place that yields no contact never attracts the premium. (See our complete Google Maps guide for how the two steps work.)
  • Job data: $3.00 per 1,000 job records delivered from live ATS feeds. A company on your list with no open roles returns zero records and costs nothing beyond the run's start event—and because the pull runs live, every delivered record reflects a role the employer currently lists as open.
  • UK company data: $0.001 per full record—company profile plus officers and persons with significant control—assembled from the official register at run time. You pay only for the companies you requested, returned complete.
  • Telegram channels: $0.0015 per post delivered to your dataset. A monitoring run that picks up twelve new posts costs twelve posts' worth, not a monthly subscription.

Two properties make those definitions work. First, the billable unit matches the buyer's unit of value rather than the vendor's unit of effort. Second, the event is verifiable: you can count rows in your dataset, inspect which records contain contacts, and reconcile the invoice line by line against delivery. A pricing model you can audit disciplines its vendor.

Why Incentives, Silent Bugs, and Breakage Matter More Than Discounts

Pricing models are incentive structures wearing invoices. Under per-row billing, a vendor makes more money by extracting more rows, so product decisions drift toward volume: wider search nets, looser filters, and tolerated duplicates. Under pay-per-result, a vendor earns more only when the buyer's yield improves, which pushes engineering attention toward the unglamorous work that actually increases usable output—filtering out parked domains, deduplicating reliably, keeping parsers current as sites evolve, and unwrapping redirect-hidden website URLs before fetching them.

What One Failed Smoke Run Taught Us About Lead Pricing

That redirect bug is not hypothetical—we ran into it directly in our own Maps engine. Our first smoke run of the real-estate variant returned 15 records and looked successful on the surface, but every record's website was a Google redirect URL, so the contact phase fetched nothing: 0 of 15 contacts, 0 emails, at a measured true cost of $0.2929. Under flat per-run pricing, that silent failure would have been the buyer's loss. That run is why our Maps pricing charges the $0.10 lead event only when a contact is actually found, and why we fixed the redirect handling in the shared engine afterwards—the repair re-test returned 5 contacts from 6 records.

Freshness and Source Breakage Under Different Billing Models

The same incentive alignment governs data freshness. A subscription database earns the exact same fee whether its records were updated yesterday or last year. A live-pull per-result vendor is paid only on delivery, and buyers only reorder runs if the returned records reflect current reality. Whenever you evaluate a data product, ask the vendor one blunt question: "What do you earn more of when my results get worse?" If the answer is "the same" or "more," the incentive points away from your interest.

Source breakage exposes that divide even faster. Every data pipeline breaks eventually because external websites restructure, platforms revise their feeds, and public registers update their interfaces. What matters is whose problem that outage becomes:

  • Under a subscription: The vendor collects full payment while output quietly degrades. Your only recourse is complaining or threatening to churn at renewal, while the repair competes with every other item on the vendor's roadmap and degradation goes unseen unless you audit records manually.
  • Under per-row pricing: Breakage can actually be profitable if a malfunctioning scraper keeps emitting billable rows of broken output.
  • Under pay-per-result: With a strict result definition, source breakage halts the vendor's revenue on the affected product immediately. Records failing the definition do not bill, empty runs do not bill, and revenue resumes only when the pipeline is repaired.

That financial feedback loop does not prevent sources from changing, but it gives the vendor an urgency to fix breakage that no SLA clause can manufacture. Ask any prospective vendor about the last time an upstream source broke and what happened to customer billing during the outage—the answer tells you which model you are truly buying.

Forecasting Spend and Auditing Your Invoice

A common objection to usage-based billing is budget unpredictability: a subscription gives you a fixed monthly figure, whereas a per-result invoice moves with yield. In practice, you can forecast spend accurately with one inexpensive test, because yields are properties of the target market rather than the run itself.

The Small-Run Forecasting Method

Instead of guessing from a sales estimate, execute the smallest realistic slice of your actual workload: one territory instead of ten, fifty companies instead of five hundred, or one channel's backfill tranche instead of the entire set. Measure the delivered records and the total charge. Your full job's budget is that observed cost scaled by the size ratio, plus a margin for variance across territories. Even better, that pilot run is not wasted forecasting overhead—it is the first tranche of usable production data. Our own buyers typically start with a smoke-scale run, audit the output, and move to full volume only after yield and quality are measured facts. A vendor who demands an annual commitment before letting you measure anything is already telling you what that measurement would show.

The Five-Minute Audit Checklist

Once a run finishes, you can verify a pay-per-result bill in four checks:

  1. Reconcile base counts: Count the delivered records in your dataset and multiply by the unit price. The math should match your charge breakdown exactly; if tiers or minimums exist, they should have been stated upfront on the Actor's page.
  2. Spot-check premium events: When you have been billed for lead premiums, sample twenty premium-charged records and verify that every single one contains the contact the premium is defined on. Per-row vendors cannot offer this check because their model has no outcome definition to test against.
  3. Inspect non-premium records: Sample records that did not trigger a premium charge. They should be genuinely contact-free—such as businesses with no published email—rather than extraction misses. If free rows are full of easily findable contacts, the pipeline is under-delivering regardless of the invoice total.
  4. Verify freshness against ground truth: Pick an entity whose current state you already know, such as a company whose director changed or a job that closed yesterday, and confirm the returned record matches reality. Our Companies House data guide walks through the same three-company check for official register data before you commit volume.

If a vendor passes all four checks, the pricing model is doing real work. If you cannot inspect and verify charges this way, "pay-per-result" is just decoration.

Contract Terms to Nail Down — and When Another Model Wins

Per-result billing shifts buyer-vendor negotiations away from headline rates and onto precise definitions, where the conversation always belonged. Regardless of which vendor you work with—us included—get five definitions written down before running at scale:

  • The result definition: State exactly what a billable record must hold. "A lead" is vague; "a place record carrying at least one publicly published email address or a contact-page URL" is enforceable.
  • The duplicate rule: Clarify whether duplicates are billed. We treat duplicates as a data-quality failure rather than a product, deduplicating on identity—name plus address, company number, or job ID—before delivery so a duplicate that slips through is not billed as a result.
  • The freshness rule: Specify when records were gathered. For live-pull products, the answer is "during your run." For compiled datasets, require a compilation date per record rather than a marketing claim on the sales page.
  • The failure bill: Confirm what an empty run costs. Under our model, it is a minimal start event and nothing else; a vendor whose empty runs are expensive is charging for extraction attempts under an outcome-shaped label.
  • The audit right: Ensure you can sample delivered records against the upstream source and dispute charges that breach the written definition. Any vendor confident in its pipeline will grant that in writing, because auditing keeps both sides honest between runs.

None of those five clauses mentions price, because most buyer disappointment in data purchasing stems from mismatched definitions rather than unit cost. A cheap record that fails to match what you thought you bought is expensive; a fairly priced record conforming to a strict written specification compounds in value every time you rerun the workflow.

When Pay-Per-Result Is NOT the Cheapest Option

Outcome billing is not universally optimal, and any vendor claiming otherwise is selling. Three genuine cases favour alternative models:

  • Very high, steady volume: If you consume millions of records every month on a stable specification, a negotiated flat arrangement will normally beat any per-unit rate—including ours. Pay-per-result wins on alignment and zero commitment, not infinite scale economics.
  • Exploratory access: When your team needs to browse freely across a broad inventory to discover what exists, an access subscription can be the right tool. Pay-per-result is built for targeted questions: these companies, this territory, this channel.
  • Data as a by-product: Where records appear as an inexpensive side output of software or a platform you already pay for, standalone per-unit pricing comparisons stop carrying much weight.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does pay-per-result differ from standard usage-based or pay-per-click pricing?

It is a stricter form of usage-based pricing. Rather than billing for attempts, API calls, or raw rows processed, charges apply only when delivered records satisfy the stated result definition—such as a record carrying a usable contact, a live job posting, a complete company filing, or a collected post.

What happens to my bill if a run returns zero results?

For most of our Actors, you pay little or nothing beyond a minimal start event ($0.00005). If a Maps territory contains no matching businesses or a company list has no open roles, there are no delivered results to bill for—that is the pricing model working as designed.

Are there monthly minimums or required subscriptions?

No. Our Actors carry no subscriptions and no monthly minimums. You pay only the per-result charges for what a run delivers, alongside Apify's platform usage for executing the run under Apify's own pricing.

How can I double-check that an invoice is accurate?

Count the records inside your delivered dataset, sample the premium-charged rows to verify each satisfies the premium definition, and reconcile those counts against the run's charge breakdown in the Apify console using the four-check audit above.

When does an annual or monthly subscription still make more sense?

A subscription makes more sense when you pull very high, steady volumes every month, when you want open-ended exploratory browsing across a wide inventory, or when the records come as a by-product of a platform you already pay for. Outside those cases, pay-per-result offers stronger alignment, full auditability, and zero commitment.

Does charging per result guarantee accurate data?

No billing model can guarantee accuracy by itself. What pay-per-result guarantees is the direction of the vendor's incentive and your right to audit deliveries. Accuracy depends on pulling from systems of record, which is why our Actors fetch live at run time rather than reselling stale stored inventories.
