Why Spain Isn't the English Market in Translation

Lead generation for Spanish local businesses fails in a specific way: the tools run, the data comes back, and the contact rate is quietly low. That's rarely a flaw in the extraction engine itself. It's that the Spanish web behaves differently from the English one at every step. Businesses are searched for in Spanish — fontanero, not plumber; clínica dental, not dental clinic; empresa de reformas, not renovation company. Their contact pages are labelled contacto, not contact. Their legally required imprint pages carry different names. Categories render in Spanish in the listing itself. And the search is run from a Spanish IP, which changes what the listing shows in the first place.

Our Google Maps Lead Extractor for Spain is the Maps engine configured for exactly that environment: a prefilled eight-term Spanish search pack across Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia, a Spanish proxy default so listings render as a local buyer sees them, and contact discovery that specifically checks Spanish /contacto pages. The Eight-Term Spanish Search Pack

A single search term under-enumerates any market, and Spanish trades fragment by vocabulary more than English ones do. The pack's eight terms cover the trades agencies ask for most, in the three metros where density justifies them:

  • Madrid: fontanero (plumber), electricista (electrician), empresa de reformas (renovation company)
  • Barcelona: clínica dental (dental clinic), restaurante (restaurant), taller mecánico (mechanic / auto workshop)
  • Valencia: clínica dental, peluquería (hair salon)

These terms are prefilled, not fixed. The Actor accepts any Spanish search terms, so a campaign for abogados in Seville or hoteles in Bilbao runs on the same engine with different inputs. The pack exists to make the first run useful without configuration, and to encode the broader lesson that a market is enumerated through its own vocabulary. Run one search per term per locality, merge, and deduplicate on name plus address — overlapping searches are how you beat the per-search result cap, in Spanish as in English. Where Spanish Contact Details Actually Live

The mechanics of contact extraction are the same everywhere — follow the listing's website link, visit the homepage and the likely contact pages, extract the public details — but the page names and habits differ. Spanish businesses publish their email on the contact page (contacto), in the footer, and on the legally required imprint (aviso legal), which in Spain is as reliably contact-bearing as Germany's imprint. The smoke test's 80% contact rate reflects that: four in five Madrid restaurants in the test carried some usable public contact, and nearly half carried an email. Compare that with the home-services figure from our English-language tests (87% contacts, 40% email) and the pattern is consistent — restaurants and appointment businesses publish more than sole-trader trades — with the Spanish configuration adding the localisation a generic extractor misses: the terms that find the businesses, the pages that hold their addresses, and the proxy that renders the listings as locals see them.

A third use of the data deserves its own mention: ratings and review counts as a prioritisation signal. A business with hundreds of reviews is a business with volume, footfall and revenue — a far better outreach target than a listing with no reviews at all. Sort the extracted list by review count before working it, and your first calls go to the businesses most likely to be able to pay for what you sell. The rating itself is a weaker signal than the count: a 4.9★ business with three reviews is a new business, not a beloved one. Volume first, rating second.

Two Failure Modes Worth Naming

First, chains and franchises: a listing for a franchised location often links to the national chain's site, whose published email belongs to head office, not the branch. Group by website domain and phone number after extraction — the record carries the fields for it — and route outreach to the decision-making location.

Second, contact forms instead of addresses: a growing share of small Spanish businesses offer only a form. There is no email to extract, and the honest output is an empty email field and a contact-page URL. Any vendor that "finds" an email for such a business invented it. Working the List: The Spanish Outreach Cadence

A list is only worth what the outreach does with it, and Spanish business culture rewards a different cadence than the Anglo one. Three adjustments matter:

  • Call, and call in Spanish. Email is the record, but the first contact that gets answered is often a phone call — the listing carries the business's own phone number precisely for that — and it should be in Spanish, because the decision-maker is the person who answers the phone, not the person who reads marketing emails. The email follows as the paper trail, in Spanish, with a one-line summary of the call.
  • Mind the hours. Spanish business hours run later than northern-European ones, and the midday pause is real for small trades: calling a plumber at 14:30 on a weekday is calling a closed shop. Late morning (10:00–13:00) and late afternoon (17:00–19:00) are the working windows for outreach; emails sent outside them sit unread until the next window anyway.
  • WhatsApp is a business channel, not a personal one. A large share of Spanish small businesses treat WhatsApp as their primary customer channel, and the listing's phone number is usually the same number. A short, polite WhatsApp message in Spanish — one line on who you are, one line on what you offer, the website link — is a legitimate first contact in Spain in a way it is not in most English-speaking markets, and it's read far more reliably than a cold email.

None of this changes the data: the list is the same list. But the yield on the list — the share of contacts that become conversations — depends on reaching businesses in the channel they actually use, at the hour they actually work, in the language they actually speak. That's the same lesson as the data itself: a market is entered on its own terms, not translated.
