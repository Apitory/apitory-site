AI Startup Jobs, Live: The Hiring Market Behind the Headlines

The AI industry's hiring is the most watched labour market in the world — and the most misread, because most of what people cite is an index. Here is what live data from the companies' own boards actually shows, and how to use it.

The AI hiring market has a centre, and it is on two systems

Every technology wave concentrates its hiring on the same few applicant tracking systems, and the AI wave is no exception. The companies defining it — frontier labs, AI infrastructure, applied-AI startups, AI biotech — run their career boards overwhelmingly on Ashby and Greenhouse. That concentration is the opportunity: instead of scraping a hundred career pages or trusting an aggregator's snapshot, you can pull the whole AI hiring market live, from the companies' own boards, through their official public APIs. Our AI Startup Jobs — Live Pack does exactly that: a curated pack of 128 AI companies, verified live with 9,454 open jobs counted at build time, priced at $3.00 per 1,000 job results.

The pack was assembled by probing 307 candidate board pairs against the five public ATS APIs and keeping only boards with live postings: Ashby contributes 84 boards and 5,681 jobs — the centre of gravity — Greenhouse 43 boards and 3,763 jobs, Workable a single board (Hugging Face, 10 jobs). No AI-startup boards with live postings were found on Lever or SmartRecruiters at build time; the cohort has consolidated onto two systems. The sector composition inside the pack is the tour a buyer actually wants: frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere, Perplexity, World Labs), AI infrastructure (Modal, Baseten, Anyscale, Fireworks AI, CoreWeave, Crusoe), applied-AI startups (Harvey, Legora, Sierra, Decagon, ElevenLabs, Synthesia, Cursor, Cognition) and AI biotech (Isomorphic Labs, insitro, Owkin, Eikon). The pack is ordered by open-job count ascending, so a capped default run samples broadly across companies before reaching the mega-boards — OpenAI alone holds 876 open roles and would otherwise swallow the whole run.

Live pull vs. index: why the freshness argument is sharpest here

Job data reaches buyers three ways — indexed databases, page scrapers, live ATS pulls — and the differences are sharpest exactly where hiring moves fastest, which is AI. An index is a snapshot: it shows postings that closed yesterday and misses the ones that opened this morning, because its crawler has not visited yet. A scraper reads career pages at run time but breaks every time a company redesigns its site. A live pull asks the ATS for a company's current openings and returns exactly what the company's own careers page is showing, because it is the same feed. When a role closes, it disappears from the feed — and from your results — immediately. The mechanics are explained in our guide to live job data from ATS APIs; the AI case is where the argument has the highest stakes, because the market it describes is the one whose participants read every signal.

The practical consequence is the diff. Run the same pack tomorrow and compare the two datasets: new records are openings, vanished records are closures, and both are real events rather than crawl artefacts. For an AI job board, the diff is the freshest content — "new roles this week" is generated, not written. For a competitor watchlist, it is the earliest signal of a rival's research investment. For a recruiting-tool vendor, it is the buying-signal feed: a hiring spike in a target account is a budget signal. And for a market researcher, the diff is the time series itself — hiring velocity by sector, remote-policy shifts, the rise and fall of role families — computed on data whose timestamps come from the employers' own systems. A closed role that disappears from the feed the day it closes is the cleanest possible proof of that freshness: the dataset never contains a job you cannot apply for.

Curation is the product: the names that were dropped on purpose

A pack is judged by what it excludes as much as what it includes, and this pack's curation decisions are worth stating plainly because they are the difference between a list that looks big and a list that means something. Four famous names were dropped during curation: xAI's Greenhouse board turned out to be an annotation-staffing pool — 300 near-identical temporary "AI Tutor" contract roles under the entity name "SpaceXSI", not the company's core hiring; Anduril's 2,600 jobs were general defense-manufacturing hiring, not an AI startup, and would have swallowed the pack's identity (a fifth of all probed volume); Epirus was defense RF/hardware, not AI-first; and one Pylon board could not be verified as the support platform of the same name. A pack that keeps those four is 13,000 jobs of noise; the 128 boards that ship are the AI cohort, verified one by one against live postings, with identity checks performed against sample posting content. Our Ashby guide, live Ashby job data, describes the same discipline on the Ashby-only cut — the slug-verification traps, the name collisions, the boards that look right and are not.

The honest gaps belong in the same paragraph. Ashby's feed carries no company display name (the board slug is the identifier) and no description-text field is missing — description text is always included — but Workable records carry no description text at all, and Ashby records carry no language or experience-level field. We return what the source actually carries rather than guessing from the description. And the pack decays: companies switch ATS and slugs die, so it is re-verified at every version build, and boards that fail are reported in the run summary rather than silently dropped.

Reading the pack as a market signal

A hiring pack is also a market instrument, and the AI pack rewards being read that way. Three readings are worth a buyer's time. Role families: the pack's titles trace the industry's shape — applied research scientists and ML engineers at the labs, forward-deployed engineers at the applied companies, infrastructure and GPU roles at the platform layer, and the newer evaluations and safety roles that did not exist as a category two years ago. Tracking which families grow fastest, quarter by quarter, is a leading indicator of where the industry's money is going before it shows up in funding announcements. Geography: the structured location fields (city, region, country, workplace type) show whether the AI industry is concentrating in San Francisco or dispersing to remote-first hiring — a question every talent buyer answers from this data rather than from headlines. Velocity: the diff between daily runs measures hiring velocity by company and by sector. A lab that opens twenty roles in a week is scaling a team; a startup whose board shrinks for a month is either consolidating or struggling. None of these readings needs a single additional data source: they are all arithmetic on the same live feed, and all of them are only trustworthy because the feed is live. An index that lags by a week turns a hiring surge into history; a live pull turns it into news while it is still actionable.

FAQ

How fresh is the data?

The Actor queries the official public ATS APIs at run time. Results reflect what each company's own careers page displays at that moment, because the careers page is served from the same system. There is no Apitory-side index between you and the source.

128 AI companies verified live at build time, holding 9,454 open jobs: frontier labs, AI infrastructure, applied-AI startups and AI biotech. The full sector breakdown is in the Actor's README; the pack is re-verified at every version build.

Four were dropped on purpose during curation: boards that turned out to be annotation-staffing pools or general mega-corporate hiring rather than the company's own AI roles. A pack that keeps them looks bigger and means less.

Can I add companies outside the pack?

Yes — pass any boards of your own in the companies input, on any of the five supported systems (Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, SmartRecruiters, Workable). The pack is the default, not the limit.

What if a company leaves its ATS?

Its board stops returning postings and the failure is reported in the run summary, not silently dropped. The pack is re-verified at every version build; the same pattern applies to your own lists — a previously active company flatlining to zero is the signature of a migration.
