Live Job Data from ATS APIs: Why the Freshest Job Listings Never Touch a Scraper

Most job-board data is scraped from rendered pages days after posting. Career-site jobs are different: the applicant tracking systems behind them publish structured feeds, and pulling from those feeds directly gives you complete, current listings. Here is how it works and when it matters.

The hidden infrastructure behind every careers page

When a company of any real size hires, it does not maintain its job listings by hand. It uses an applicant tracking system (ATS) — Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, SmartRecruiters, Workable and their competitors — and the careers page on the company website is a window into that system. The listings are created once, inside the ATS, and displayed everywhere from there.

This architecture has a consequence most data buyers never hear about: the ATS platforms expose the same structured job data through public, documented feeds, because their customers' own career sites, job-board integrations and widgets are built on top of them. The data a scraper struggles to reconstruct from HTML — title, department, location, workplace type, the full description, the application URL — is available as clean structured records at the source.

Live pull vs. scraping vs. indexed databases: three very different products

Job data reaches buyers through three routes, and they are not interchangeable:

  • Indexed databases crawl the web's career pages on a cycle, store what they find, and sell access to the store. The buyer gets breadth, but the data is only as fresh as the last crawl, records linger after the underlying job closes, and fields the crawler could not parse are silently wrong or missing. If you have ever bought job data and found listings for roles that closed weeks ago, you have met this model.
  • Page scrapers fetch career pages at run time and parse the HTML. Fresher than an index, but fragile: every company's page is a different template, JavaScript rendering hides content, and a redesign breaks the parser. Scrapers also inherit a structural blind spot — they can only see companies whose pages they have been pointed at and built for.
  • Live ATS pulls ask the ATS for a company's current openings at run time and return exactly what the company's own careers page is displaying at that moment — because it is the same feed. There is no index to go stale and no HTML to parse. When a role closes, it disappears from the feed, and from your results, immediately.

What a live-pulled job record contains

Because the record comes from the system the employer itself maintains, the fields are the employer's own:

  • Title, department and team — as structured in the ATS, not guessed from page text.
  • Location and workplace type — office location, remote/hybrid flags as the employer set them.
  • The full job description — the complete posting text, not a snippet.
  • The application URL — the canonical link into the employer's own application flow.
  • Posting and update timestamps — from the ATS, which is what makes genuine change detection possible: run the same company list tomorrow and diff the two datasets, and the differences are real events — new roles opened, roles closed — not crawl artefacts.

That last property is the one buyers underrate. An index can tell you what it believes is open. A live pull, repeated, tells you what changed — and for use cases like competitor hiring signals, market mapping and niche job boards, the changes are the product.

Who buys live job data, and for what

Niche job boards. A board for one profession, one technology or one region needs listings that are complete for its niche and dead-listing-free, because a board that sends applicants to closed roles burns its only asset — trust. Live pulls from a curated company list give a small board fresher inventory than it could ever crawl. We wrote a separate piece on building a niche job board on live data.

Recruiting and staffing firms. A firm's own target-account list is a company list. Pulling those companies' live openings turns "check 200 career pages" into one scheduled run, and the diff between runs is a call list: this account just opened three engineering roles.

Market and competitor intelligence. Hiring is a public signal of strategy. Structured, timestamped openings across a defined company set let analysts count, classify and track that signal without maintaining scrapers.

Lead generation. A company that is hiring for a role is a company with a budget and a problem. Live openings, filtered by department, are a buying-signal feed for anyone selling into that function.

The limits of the live-pull model — stated plainly

Live ATS pulls only work for companies that use a supported ATS and publish their openings through it. That is a large and growing universe — the five platforms our Actor covers host the career sites of a substantial share of technology and growth companies — but it is not every employer. Companies on unsupported systems, companies that hide listings behind their own portals, and very small employers listing only on job boards are outside the feed's reach. A live-pull Actor also needs to be told which companies to pull: it is a precision instrument for a defined company list, not a discovery engine for "all jobs everywhere". Buyers who need discovery across the whole web still need an index — and should buy it knowing the freshness trade they are making.

There is also a scope choice inside the model. Some buyers only care about one ATS — most often Greenhouse, the default for a large slice of the tech market. For them we ship a single-system cut, Greenhouse Jobs — Live API, with the same live-pull mechanics and the same $3.00 per 1,000 jobs pricing, without the multi-system surface they will never use.

The five platforms, from a buyer's point of view

Buyers rarely choose an ATS — the employers on their list already chose. But the mix of platforms on your list shapes what you get, so a short field guide helps. Greenhouse is the default for a large share of technology and growth companies; its boards are consistently structured, which is why our Greenhouse-only cut exists. Ashby is the newer platform winning fast-growing startups, and its listings tend to carry rich location and workplace detail because its customers configure them carefully. Lever has a long installed base across tech and professional services. SmartRecruiters skews toward larger and enterprise employers, where volumes per company are higher. Workable is strong among small and mid-size companies internationally. The operational point is not which is "best" — it is that a multi-system pull treats your company list as one market across all five, normalising the records into a single schema, so a company switching platforms does not fall out of your data. In the verification run for our multi-system Actor, a single pull returned 2,044 live jobs across the five platforms with counts matching the sources exactly — that normalisation, maintained as the platforms evolve, is most of what you are paying for.

From pulls to products: three patterns that work

The watchlist. A fixed company list, pulled on a schedule, diffed each run. This is the recruiter's and the sales team's pattern: the deliverable is not the dataset but the change — this account opened its first sales role, that account's engineering team doubled its openings in a month. Watchlists work because hiring is a budgeted, time-bound intention; a company does not open a role it does not mean to fill.

The board. A niche job board is a watchlist published: the same pull, classified into a taxonomy and presented to an audience. We devoted a full article to building a niche job board on live data, including why dead listings — structurally impossible with feed pulls — are the trust issue that decides whether boards live or die.

The signal feed. For analysts and data teams, the pull is an input, not a product: openings classified by function and seniority become a time series — hiring velocity by company, by sector, by region — that feeds models of growth, expansion and strategy. The requirement that makes or breaks this pattern is timestamp integrity: because records come from the employer's own system with its own posting dates, the series reflects real events rather than the artefacts of a crawler's schedule.

Judging a job-data sample before you buy

Job data is verifiable goods, and a ten-minute sample audit settles most vendor questions. Pick five records at random and open their application URLs: every one should resolve to a live posting on the employer's own system — a single "this position has been filled" page in five is a freshness confession. Check the posting dates against the employer's careers page for two companies you know: feed-pulled data will match exactly, because it is the same data; indexed data will drift by days. Look at the fields a parser would struggle with — workplace type, department, location — and confirm they are populated consistently across companies on different platforms; that consistency is the signature of a normalised feed pipeline rather than five scrapers in a trench coat. Finally, count: run the same company list against the employers' own pages and compare totals. Our own standard, from the verification run, is exact agreement — 2,044 records against 2,044 live postings — and it is the standard to hold any vendor to, because with feed data there is no honest reason for the numbers to differ.

Keeping the input alive: company-list operations

The Actor's input — your company list — is a living asset, and its maintenance is the one operational duty the live-pull model leaves with the buyer. It is light work, but it is real, and boards and signal products live or die by it. Three events drive the maintenance. Companies enter your universe: a startup raises its first round and starts hiring, a firm expands into your region, a new player appears in your niche. Discovery of entrants is editorial work — funding announcements, association lists, your own market watching — and a quarterly review that adds them keeps your coverage honest. Companies migrate platforms: growing companies switch ATS as they scale, and a board token that returned forty jobs last quarter can return zero after a migration — not because the company stopped hiring, but because it now hires through a different system. The operational signature is unmistakable (a healthy company flatlining to zero), and the fix is a two-minute check of its careers page and a token update. A monthly scan of your list for exactly this pattern — previously active, now zero — catches migrations before your users do. Companies exit: acquisitions and shutdowns end hiring; leaving dead companies on the list costs nothing in per-result pricing (they deliver no records), but removing them keeps your coverage statistics truthful. The total burden is an hour or two a month for a list of several hundred companies — a rounding error next to the parser-maintenance burden of the scraping model, and it is spent on market knowledge rather than on plumbing.

Terms and posture: why feed pulls are the defensible route

Buyers increasingly have to answer for how their data was obtained, and job data is an area where the routes differ sharply in defensibility. The ATS feeds exist to distribute the employers' listings: the platforms publish them so that career sites, widgets and integrations can display the openings. Pulling a company's openings from the feed its own ATS publishes is using the data through the channel built for distributing it, at the employer's own level of openness — every listing in the feed is one the employer is actively advertising to the public. Contrast the scraping route, where the buyer's data exists because a crawler reconstructed what a website's terms may well prohibit copying at scale, and the index route, where provenance is a shrug. For a data product you intend to build a business on — a board, a signal feed, a recruiting tool — the provenance story is part of the asset. "We pull live from the applicant tracking systems' public feeds, the same source as the employers' own career pages" is a sentence you can say to customers, partners and platforms without flinching. In a market where data provenance is becoming a procurement question, that sentence has commercial value beyond its ethics.

FAQ

How fresh is "live", exactly?

The Actor queries the ATS feeds at run time. The results reflect what each company's careers page displays at that moment, because the careers page is served from the same system. There is no Apitory-side index or cache between you and the source.

Which ATS platforms are covered?

Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, SmartRecruiters and Workable in the multi-system Actor. A Greenhouse-only version is available if your company list lives entirely on Greenhouse.

Can I discover companies I do not already know?

No — and that is by design. You supply the company list; the Actor returns those companies' live openings. Discovery across the whole web is a different product (an indexed database), with the freshness costs described above.

How do I detect new and closed jobs?

Run the same company list on a schedule and diff consecutive datasets on the job's identity (company plus job ID or application URL). New IDs are openings; vanished IDs are closures. Because both snapshots are live pulls, the diff reflects real changes rather than crawl timing.

What does it cost to monitor 500 companies?

You pay per job record delivered, not per company checked: $3.00 per 1,000 results. A run over 500 companies that returns 800 open jobs costs about $2.40 in Actor charges. Companies with no openings cost nothing beyond the run's start event.

Is the full job description included?

Yes. Records carry the complete posting text as published by the employer in the ATS, along with department, location, workplace type and the application URL.
