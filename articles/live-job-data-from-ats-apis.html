Pulling Live Job Data Directly from ATS Feeds: Architecture, Economics, and Quality Checks

TL;DR: Live ATS pulls query five applicant tracking systems at run time, matching employers' careers pages without HTML scrapers or stale indexes. Supply a verified company list, diff scheduled runs for openings and closures, and pay $0.003 per delivered job record.

Most job-board data is scraped from rendered web pages days after a role goes live. Career-site jobs work differently: the applicant tracking systems behind those pages publish structured feeds, and pulling from those feeds directly delivers complete, current listings. Here is how that architecture works, how to operate and audit it, and when it is the right fit.

Why Career Pages Are Already Structured APIs

When a company of any real size hires, it does not maintain its job listings by hand. It runs an applicant tracking system (ATS) — Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, SmartRecruiters, Workable, and their competitors — and the careers page on the company website is simply a window into that system. Listings are created once inside the ATS and displayed everywhere else from there.

This setup carries a consequence most data buyers never hear about: the ATS platforms expose that same structured job data through public, documented feeds because their customers' own career sites, job-board integrations, and widgets are built on top of them. The fields an HTML scraper struggles to reconstruct — title, department, location, workplace type, the full description, and the application URL — are already sitting at the source as clean, structured records.

Data Provenance and Terms Posture

Buyers increasingly have to answer for how their job data was obtained, and sourcing routes differ sharply in defensibility. ATS feeds exist to distribute employers' openings: the platforms publish them so that career sites, widgets, and integrations can display active roles. Pulling a company's listings from the feed its own ATS publishes uses the channel built for distributing that data, at the employer's own level of openness — every listing in the feed is one the employer is actively advertising to the public.

That posture stands in sharp contrast to scraping, where data exists because a crawler reconstructed what a website's terms may prohibit copying at scale, and to indexed databases, where provenance is a shrug. For a data product you intend to build a business on — a job board, a signal feed, or a recruiting tool — the provenance story is part of the asset. Being able to state plainly to customers, partners, and platforms that you pull live from applicant tracking systems' public feeds, the same source as the employers' own career pages, carries real commercial value in procurement conversations beyond its ethics.

Comparing Job-Data Sourcing Models: Feeds, Scrapers, and Indexes

Job data reaches buyers through three distinct routes, and those routes are not interchangeable:

  • Indexed databases crawl career pages across the web on a cycle, store what they find, and sell access to that store. Buyers get breadth, but the data is only as fresh as the last crawl, listings linger after the underlying job closes, and fields the crawler failed to parse are silently wrong or missing. Anyone who has bought job data and found roles that closed weeks earlier has run into this model.
  • Page scrapers fetch career pages at run time and parse the HTML. They are fresher than an index, yet fragile: every employer's site uses a different template, JavaScript rendering hides content, and a page redesign breaks the parser. Scrapers also share a structural blind spot — they can only see the companies whose pages they have been pointed at and built for.
  • Live ATS pulls ask the ATS for a company's current openings at run time and return exactly what the employer's own careers page displays at that moment, because both draw from the same feed. There is no index to go stale and no HTML to parse. When a role closes, it vanishes from the feed — and from your results — immediately.

Given a list of companies, it queries the public feeds of five ATS platforms — Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, SmartRecruiters, and Workable — and returns the openings those companies have live right now at $3.00 per 1,000 job results. In our verification run, it pulled 2,044 jobs across all five systems, and the totals matched the live sources exactly because there is no intermediate copy for the counts to disagree with.

Scope Boundaries and the Single-System Alternative

Live ATS pulls only work for employers that use a supported ATS and publish their openings through it. That covers a large and growing universe — the five supported platforms host the career sites of a substantial share of technology and growth companies — yet it does not cover every employer. Companies running unsupported systems, firms that keep listings behind their own portals, and very small businesses posting solely on external job boards sit outside the feed's reach.

A live-pull Actor also has to be told which companies to query: it is a precision instrument for a defined company list, not a discovery engine for all jobs everywhere. Buyers who need open-web discovery still need an indexed database, and should purchase one with clear eyes about the freshness trade-off.

Scope also varies inside the live-pull model itself. Many buyers only care about a single ATS — most often Greenhouse, the default across a large slice of the tech market. For those buyers we offer a single-system cut, Greenhouse Jobs — Live API, which uses the same live-pull mechanics and the same $3.00 per 1,000 jobs pricing without the multi-system surface they will never touch.

Inside the Normalized Schema and Sample Output

Because each record comes straight from the system the employer itself maintains, the values reflect the employer's own entries rather than guesses extracted from rendered HTML:

  • Title, department, and team — captured as structured in the ATS, not inferred from page text.
  • Location and workplace type — office locations plus remote or hybrid flags as configured by the employer.
  • The full job description — the complete posting text rather than a truncated snippet.
  • The application URL — the canonical link directly into the employer's application flow.
  • Posting and update timestamps — taken from the ATS, making genuine change detection possible: run the same company list tomorrow and diff the two datasets, and every difference reflects a real event — new openings or closed roles — rather than a crawl artefact.

That timestamp property is the one buyers most often underrate. An index tells you what it believes is open. Repeated live pulls tell you what changed — and for competitor hiring signals, market mapping, and niche job boards, the changes are the product.

How the Five Platforms Behave for Buyers

Buyers rarely pick an ATS — the employers on their target list have already made that choice. Still, the mix of systems on your list shapes the output you receive, so a short field guide helps:

  • Greenhouse is the default for a large share of technology and growth companies, and its boards are consistently structured — which is why our Greenhouse-only Actor exists.
  • Ashby is the newer platform gaining ground among fast-growing startups, and its listings tend to include rich location and workplace detail because customers configure those fields carefully.
  • Lever has a long installed base across technology and professional services firms.
  • SmartRecruiters skews toward larger and enterprise employers, where opening volumes per company tend to be higher.
  • Workable is strong among small and mid-size companies internationally.

Operationally, the point is not which ATS is "best" — it is that a multi-system pull treats your company list as a single market across all five, normalizing records into one schema so an employer switching platforms never drops out of your pipeline. In the verification run for our multi-system Actor, a single pull returned 2,044 live jobs across the five platforms with counts matching the sources exactly; maintaining that normalization as platforms evolve is most of what you pay for.

Probing Candidate Boards in Practice: What Breaks Without Verification

Live pulling only works when the boards behind your company list are real, and building curated lists has taught us to verify every candidate slug directly against the ATS APIs rather than trusting directories at face value.

When we built curated packs on this engine, we probed every candidate board against the ATS APIs: 577 Greenhouse slugs in one pack build, 639 Ashby slugs in another, and 307 cross-ATS candidates in a third. Those probes caught false matches that a directory would never flag: a "wise" board that belonged to an unrelated field-sales firm, a "crisp" board that belonged to a Dutch grocery-delivery company rather than the chat platform, and an "xai" board that turned out to be an annotation-staffing pool of 300 near-identical temporary roles rather than core hiring. We dropped all of them.

We also verified empty responses before trusting them: when 48 Lever candidates returned no live postings, we sanity-checked the Lever endpoint against the known-good binance board, which returned HTTP 200 and real postings, proving those zeroes were genuine.

Keeping Your Company List Alive

Your company list is the Actor's input, and maintaining it is the one ongoing operational responsibility the live-pull model leaves with the buyer. It is light work, yet real, and job boards and signal products succeed or fail by how cleanly they handle it. Three recurring events drive list maintenance:

  1. Companies enter your universe. A startup raises its first round and begins hiring, a company expands into your region, or a new competitor enters your niche. Discovering entrants is editorial work — tracking funding announcements, association lists, and your own market — and a quarterly review keeps coverage honest.
  2. Companies migrate platforms. Growing firms switch ATS as they scale, so a board token that returned forty jobs last quarter can return zero after a migration — not because hiring stopped, but because the employer moved to another system. The signature is unmistakable (a healthy company flatlining to zero), and the fix is a two-minute check of its careers page followed by a token update. A monthly scan for previously active accounts that dropped to zero catches migrations before your users notice.
  3. Companies exit. Acquisitions and shutdowns halt hiring. Leaving defunct companies on the list costs nothing under per-result pricing because they deliver no records, but removing them keeps your coverage metrics truthful.

For a list of several hundred companies, total maintenance takes an hour or two a month — a rounding error beside the parser-maintenance burden of scraping, and time spent on market knowledge rather than plumbing.

Auditing a Job-Data Sample in Ten Minutes

Job data is verifiable goods, and a ten-minute sample audit settles most questions before you commit to a vendor:

  1. Test live links. Pick five records at random and open their application URLs. Every one should resolve to a live posting on the employer's own system; even a single "this position has been filled" page out of five is a freshness confession.
  2. Compare posting dates. Check posting timestamps against the employer's careers page for two companies you know. Feed-pulled records match exactly because they come from the same feed, while indexed data drifts by days.
  3. Inspect parser-sensitive fields. Check workplace type, department, and location across companies on different platforms. Consistent population across all five systems is the signature of a normalized feed pipeline rather than five scrapers in a trench coat.
  4. Check exact counts. Run the same company list against the employers' own careers pages and compare totals. Our standard from the verification run is exact agreement — 2,044 records against 2,044 live postings — and that is the bar any vendor should meet, since with feed data there is no honest reason for totals to differ.

From Raw Snapshots to Change Feeds: Buyer Workflows and Products

To see how the mechanics work in practice, follow one morning run end to end. A recruiting firm maintains a watchlist of 300 target companies spread across the five ATS platforms. Each morning's scheduled run submits that list, the Actor queries each company's board, and the firm receives a single dataset — say 1,140 records on a typical day, costing about $3.42 in result charges.

The firm's downstream system diffs today's dataset against yesterday's on company plus job identity. The morning deliverable is not the 1,140 rows; it is the eleven changes: six new openings (two of them at accounts with no prior hiring, meaning the call list writes itself), three closures, and two role re-posts. Nobody visited a careers page, and nobody fixed a broken parser.

That exact output supports three distinct product patterns:

  • The watchlist. A fixed company list pulled on a schedule and diffed after every run. Recruiters and sales teams rely on this pattern because the deliverable is the delta — this account just opened its first sales role, or that engineering team doubled its openings in a month. Watchlists work because hiring reflects a budgeted, time-bound intent.
  • The niche job board. A job board is a watchlist published: the same pull, classified into a taxonomy and presented to candidates. We explored this in building a niche job board on live data, including why eliminating dead listings — structurally impossible to miss with live feed pulls — is the trust issue that decides whether boards survive.
  • The signal feed. For analysts and data teams, the pull is an input rather than a finished product. Openings classified by function and seniority become a time series — tracking hiring velocity by company, sector, and region — that feeds models of growth and strategy. Timestamp integrity makes or breaks this pattern: because dates come directly from the employer's ATS, the series captures real hiring events rather than a crawler's schedule.

Who Uses Live Job Data — and Why

  • Niche job boards: A board serving one profession, technology, or region needs complete coverage within its niche and zero dead listings, because sending applicants to closed roles destroys its core asset — trust. Pulling live from a curated company list gives a focused board fresher inventory than crawling ever could, a workflow we unpack further in our guide to building a niche job board on live data.
  • Recruiting and staffing firms: A firm's target-account roster is already a company list. Pulling live openings turns checking 200 career pages into one scheduled run, and the diff between runs becomes an immediate call list when an account opens three engineering roles.
  • Market and competitor intelligence teams: Hiring is a public signal of strategy. Structured, timestamped openings across a defined set of companies let analysts count, classify, and track those shifts without maintaining scrapers.
  • Lead-generation teams: A company hiring for a specific role is a company with an approved budget and an active problem. Live openings filtered by department act as a buying-signal feed for anyone selling into that function.

Common Questions About Live ATS Pulls

What does "live" mean in practice for freshness?

The Actor queries the ATS feeds at run time, so results reflect what each company's careers page displays at that exact moment because both come from the same system. There is no Apitory-side index or cache sitting between your run and the source.

Which applicant tracking systems are supported?

The multi-system Actor covers Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, SmartRecruiters, and Workable. A dedicated Greenhouse-only version is also available when your entire company list lives on Greenhouse.

Will the Actor discover companies I have not listed?

No — and that is intentional. You provide the company list and the Actor returns those companies' live openings. Discovering companies across the entire web belongs to a different product category (an indexed database), carrying the freshness trade-offs outlined earlier.

How do I track newly opened and closed positions over time?

Schedule the same company list to run regularly and diff consecutive datasets on each job's identity (company plus job ID or application URL). New IDs represent openings and vanished IDs represent closures; because both snapshots are live pulls, the diff captures genuine changes rather than crawl timing.

What does a run across 500 companies cost?

Charges are based on job records delivered rather than companies checked, at $3.00 per 1,000 results. A run across 500 companies that returns 800 open roles costs about $2.40 in Actor charges, while companies with zero openings incur no per-record fees beyond the run's start event.

Do records contain the full job description?

Yes — records carry the complete posting text published by the employer in the ATS, alongside department, location, workplace type, and the application URL.
