Lead Generation

Turning Google Maps Into a Live Local Lead Pipeline

TL;DR: Google Maps offers structured local business listings but no emails. Extract cheap place records first, filter out closed or off-target rows, and pay the lead fee only when website enrichment finds a public contact.

Building a repeatable source of local business leads from Google Maps comes down to understanding what the map actually holds, how extraction and enrichment work together, what the run costs, and how to turn raw listings into outreach-ready records without paying for dead data.

Two Stages: Market Mapping Before Website Enrichment

Almost every local business on earth maintains a presence on Google Maps, whether it manages that presence actively or not. A single listing carries the business name, category, address, phone number, opening hours, rating, review count, and—most useful for outreach—a link to its website. No other single directory covers as many plumbers, dentists, restaurants, salons, clinics, workshops, and retailers in one consistent shape.

That shared structure is why Maps works so well for prospecting. A chamber-of-commerce directory gives you one format per town, and a trade association gives you members only. Google Maps returns the same fields for a roofer in Dallas and a roofer in Dortmund, so a single extraction workflow can assemble a list for any trade in any city on demand instead of relying on a static list that began aging the day it was compiled.

The catch is that Maps is designed for people browsing a screen, not for data pipelines. The records are there, but they are paginated, rate-limited, and loaded one screenful at a time. Turning Maps into a lead source means pulling search results—a trade paired with a location—into a structured dataset, and then enriching the qualifying rows with public contact details from the businesses' own websites.

Effective Maps lead generation separates two distinct jobs that are often lumped together:

  • Stage 1 — Place extraction: You run a search for a trade and location, collect every matching listing, and save the structured fields. This step is fast and inexpensive, and it maps your addressable market by showing how many businesses of that type operate in the area.
  • Stage 2 — Contact enrichment: For listings that have a website, the enrichment step visits the business's own site and pulls public contact details such as email addresses, extra phone numbers, social links, and contact-page URLs. Because fetching and parsing arbitrary websites is the costly step, it should run—and be billed—only on listings that pass your criteria.

Splitting the pipeline protects your budget. Enriching every raw listing means paying to crawl websites for permanently closed businesses, duplicate pins, and companies outside your target profile. Filtering the place list first—by status, category, rating, or review count—ensures enrichment spend goes strictly toward businesses you would actually contact.

That two-stage separation is also how our Google Maps Contacts & Leads Actor is priced: $0.004 per place extracted, plus a $0.10 lead fee charged only when a usable public contact is found. A place that yields no contact costs the place fee and nothing more—an outcome-aligned model that this guide uses throughout.

Fields You Get and What a Real Record Looks Like

Each extracted place record gives you the core listing fields directly from Google Maps:

  • Business name and category: The primary category the business lists itself under, such as "Dental clinic" or "HVAC contractor".
  • Address and coordinates: The full postal address alongside latitude and longitude, which simplifies territory mapping and duplicate detection.
  • Phone number: The primary number displayed on the listing, typically the business's main line.
  • Website: The business's own site, serving as the bridge to enrichment where public email addresses sit in footers and contact pages.
  • Rating and review count: A quick signal of activity and standing—hundreds of recent reviews point to an active business, while none may indicate a dormant listing.
  • Opening hours and business status: Including permanently-closed flags, which belong in your dataset specifically so you can filter those businesses out deliberately.

What a Google Maps listing does not include is an email address. Anyone promising email addresses "from Google Maps" is running the second step: visiting each business's website and extracting the contact information the business published there for customers. Knowing where that data originates matters for both accuracy and compliance.

When contact enrichment runs against a business's own website, it adds public email addresses, additional phone numbers, social links, and contact-page links to the record.   • Static database subscriptions: You pay a monthly fee whether you use the data or not, and every record ages from the day it was compiled. That suits broad, slow-moving markets, not "every business of this trade in this city, this week".

How to Build, Filter, and Segment a List

Executing that workflow reliably across any trade and city comes down to five operational habits:

Frame searches the way customers search

Google Maps responds to natural-language queries such as "emergency plumber in Leeds", "dentist in Berlin", or "roofing contractor near Austin". Results shift with phrasing, so start with the plain trade name plus city that local businesses optimise for. In dense markets, a single query hits result caps, so run multiple adjacent phrasings or pair trade-and-city combinations and merge them afterwards.

Collect raw places with closed listings intact

Expect the initial extraction to include duplicate pins (the same company listed under minor name variations), a handful of mis-categorised listings, and closed businesses. Keep the business status field visible rather than stripping closed businesses silently during extraction—if a territory's active count changes later, knowing how many closed avoids misreading the market.

Filter and deduplicate at zero enrichment cost

Before running contact enrichment, drop permanently closed businesses, remove duplicates using name plus address or coordinates—catching variants like "Smith & Sons Plumbing", "Smith and Sons Plumbing Ltd", and a separate pin at a yard address—and apply your buyer criteria. Drop low ratings or low review counts if you sell to established firms, or keep low-review listings if you target newer businesses. Filtering is free; enrichment is what costs money.

Enrich the survivors and evaluate yield by trade

Run website contact extraction only on the filtered set. Email publishing rates vary widely by trade, which is why paying the $0.10 lead fee only when a contact is found outperforms per-row billing while the $0.004 place fee covers discovery.   • Google Maps Dentist Leads uses a five-term dental pack. In our Berlin smoke test, 93% of places carried contacts and 87% carried an email, reflecting how consistently dental practices publish contact details.

Verify, segment, and store provenance

Before importing into a campaign, syntax-check email addresses, remove role addresses if your outreach requires named contacts, and segment using the listing metrics you already captured—rating band, review count, and neighbourhood. Attach the source query and extraction date to every row inside your CRM or outreach tool so teams can refresh lists cleanly instead of starting over from scratch.

Edge Cases in Extraction: What Happened With Redirect URLs

Even with a clean workflow, step two—fetching arbitrary business websites—exposes real-world quirks in how Google Maps formats outbound links. For a detailed look at how website contact extraction works and what can break, read our guide on extracting contacts from Google Maps.

We hit one of those quirks first-hand during our build and repair work. On 2026-11-26, during a smoke run for a related build, we found that Google Maps intermittently returns a place's website not as the direct URL, but as a Google redirect URL formatted as /url?q=http://...&opi=.... Because our contact-extraction phase could not fetch that shape, affected runs silently finished marked as "success" with place records only—0 contacts and 0 emails—while still consuming the full proxy spend. Since the underlying engine is shared, the issue affected the entire Maps Actor family rather than a single Actor.

We fixed the bug by unwrapping the redirect before enrichment: whenever a website link contains /url?q=, we extract the q parameter, URL-decode it, and pass it through only when it resolves to an http(s) target; direct URLs, missing values, and non-http targets pass through unchanged. We unit-tested the shipped function across 10 cases and all 10 behaved as intended. In the re-run smoke test after the fix—searching for a pizzeria in Madrid capped at 6 places—the run returned 6 places, 5 with a website, 0 redirect URLs, 4 contacts found, and 3 with an email address, at a measured platform cost of $0.1204.

Common Pitfalls, Data Hygiene, and Compliance

Most underperforming Maps campaigns fail for predictable operational reasons rather than data scarcity:

  • Chasing list volume over freshness: A 5,000-row list of mixed age routinely loses to a 500-row list pulled this week because businesses move, close, and change phone numbers constantly. Live extraction at run time beats resold static indexes, and refreshing before major campaigns keeps details aligned with live listings.
  • Enriching before filtering: Paying to crawl websites for closed businesses, duplicate listings, and out-of-area firms wastes budget on records you will discard anyway.
  • Relying on a single query in dense markets: One search misses listings behind result caps; adjacent phrasings plus name-and-address deduplication is the proper way to enumerate a city.
  • Ignoring phone numbers in phone-first trades: Filtering strictly for email shrinks your market unnecessarily when businesses list their main phone number specifically to receive customer calls.
  • Dropping provenance or closed-status records: Without source queries and extraction dates, lists rot because nobody knows how to refresh them. Keep closed businesses in a separate tab rather than deleting them so you never pitch dead companies while retaining territory counts over time.

On the compliance side, extracting publicly displayed business information and publicly published website contact details is common practice, though legal requirements vary by jurisdiction, platform terms, and usage—and this guide is not legal advice. Our Actors collect public listing data alongside contact details businesses publish on their own websites for customers. Use those details strictly for relevant business outreach, honour opt-out requests immediately, and follow the privacy and anti-spam laws where you send. A clean list used carelessly is still spam; a clean list used precisely builds pipeline.

Limits of Maps Data and Layering Your Prospecting Stack

Google Maps excels at local, customer-facing businesses with a physical or service presence. It is the wrong source for finding individual professionals without listed premises, businesses that deliberately remain unlisted, or private-individual contact records—none of that belongs on Maps or should be scraped elsewhere.

Maps also cannot show corporate attributes that live outside a storefront listing, such as company age, ownership structure, headcount, or technology stack. In practice, Maps works best as the local layer at the base of a multi-source stack:

  • Local presence and reachability (Google Maps): Answers who operates in the territory and how to contact them.
  • Corporate register data: Turns "a dental practice in Leeds" into "a company incorporated in 2019, two directors, active status". For UK prospecting, layer our guide to Companies House data on top of your Maps list.
  • Hiring signals: For companies large enough to recruit through an applicant tracking system, live job data from ATS APIs adds active growth signals.

Together, those layers answer the core questions of any B2B prospecting operation: who exists locally, whether they are real and trading, whether they are expanding, and how to reach them.

Common Questions About Maps Lead Extraction

Where do the email addresses actually come from?

Not from Google Maps itself. Maps listings carry names, categories, addresses, phone numbers, websites, ratings, and opening hours. Emails come from the enrichment step, which visits each business's own website and reads the contact details published there for customers.

How up to date are the extracted records?

Records reflect the live Google Maps listing and the business's live website at the exact moment the run executes, because extraction pulls from the source at run time instead of a stored database. Because business details change continuously, re-run before major outreach pushes.

What happens if a listing has no public contact details?

You pay only the $0.004 place fee (plus the $0.00005 Actor start fee per run). The $0.10 lead fee is billed only when a public contact is found, so dead-end listings do not trigger enrichment charges.

Can I cover multiple cities or multiple trades in one workflow?

Yes. Run separate searches per trade-and-city pair—or overlapping query phrasings for a single dense market—and merge the exported JSON, CSV, or Excel datasets, deduplicating on business name plus address or coordinates.

Is it legal to collect business leads this way?

While gathering publicly displayed business details and publicly published website contacts is widely practiced, rules depend on your local jurisdiction, applicable terms, and how you send outreach. Always comply with local anti-spam and privacy regulations and process opt-outs immediately.
