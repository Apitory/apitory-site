Google Maps Lead Generation: The Complete Guide

How to turn Google Maps into a structured, repeatable source of local business leads — what data you can actually get, how the extraction works, what it costs, and how to use the results without wasting money on dead records.

Why Google Maps is a lead source at all

Almost every local business on earth maintains a presence on Google Maps, whether it manages that presence actively or not. A listing carries the business name, its category, its address, its phone number, its opening hours, its rating and review count, and — most valuable for outreach — a link to its website. No other single directory covers as many plumbers, dentists, restaurants, salons, clinics, workshops and retailers in one consistent shape.

That consistency is what makes Maps useful for lead generation. A chamber-of-commerce directory gives you one format per town. A trade association gives you members only. Google Maps gives you the same fields for a roofer in Dallas and a roofer in Dortmund, which means a single extraction process can build a list for any trade in any city, on demand, instead of buying a static list that started going stale the day it was compiled.

The catch is that Maps is built for humans looking at a screen, not for pipelines. The data is there, but it is paginated, rate-limited and presented a screenful at a time. Lead generation from Maps is therefore an extraction problem: how to pull the listings for a search — a trade plus a place — into a spreadsheet or dataset, then enrich the rows that matter with contact details from the businesses' own public websites.

What data a Maps listing actually contains

A typical extracted place record includes:

  • Business name and category — the primary category the business lists itself under, such as "Dental clinic" or "HVAC contractor".
  • Address and coordinates — the full postal address plus latitude and longitude, which makes territory mapping and duplicate detection straightforward.
  • Phone number — the number shown on the listing, usually the business's main line.
  • Website — the business's own site, which is the bridge to contact enrichment: contact pages and footers are where public email addresses live.
  • Rating and review count — a fast quality and activity signal. A business with hundreds of recent reviews is trading; a business with none may be dormant.
  • Opening hours and status — including permanently-closed flags, which you want in the data precisely so you can filter those businesses out.

What a listing does not contain is an email address. Anyone who promises emails "from Google Maps" is actually doing the second step — visiting the business's website and extracting the publicly published contact details. That distinction matters for both accuracy and legality: the email comes from the business's own site, where the business itself published it for customers to use.

The two-step model: places first, contacts second

Serious Maps lead generation always separates two jobs that are often confused:

  1. Place extraction. Run a search (trade + location), collect every matching listing, and store the structured fields. This is cheap and fast, and it defines your market: how many businesses of this type exist in this area.
  2. Contact enrichment. For each place with a website, visit the site and extract the public contact details — email addresses, additional phone numbers, contact-page links. This is the expensive step, because it involves fetching and parsing arbitrary websites, so it should be charged and run only against places that pass your filters.

Separating the steps is what keeps the economics sane. If you enrich every raw listing, you pay to fetch websites for closed businesses, duplicates and businesses outside your real target profile. If you filter the place list first — by rating, review count, status, category — you only pay enrichment prices for rows you would actually contact.

This is also how our own Google Maps Contacts & Leads Actor is priced: $0.004 per place extracted, and a $0.10 lead charge only when the record actually carries a usable public contact. A place that yields no contact costs you the place price and nothing more. We think that is the only fair way to price enrichment, and it is the model this guide assumes throughout.

Step by step: building a lead list from Maps

1. Define the search the way a customer would

Maps searches are natural-language queries: "emergency plumber in Leeds", "dentist in Berlin", "roofing contractor near Austin". The results differ by phrasing, so pick the phrasing your target businesses optimise for — usually the plain trade name plus the city. If you need wider coverage, run several adjacent phrasings and deduplicate on name plus address afterwards; overlapping searches are the standard way to beat the per-search result cap.

2. Extract the places

Run the extraction and collect the full place records. Expect the raw list to contain duplicates (the same business under slightly different names), a few mis-categorised businesses, and some closed ones. That is normal; the next step exists for exactly this reason.

3. Filter before you enrich

Apply your buying criteria to the place data first: drop permanently closed businesses, drop duplicates, drop ratings or review counts below your threshold if you sell to established businesses — or keep the low-review ones if you sell to new businesses, in which case a low review count is your targeting signal. The point is that the filter decision is yours and it is free; enrichment is the part that costs money.

4. Enrich the survivors

Run contact extraction against the filtered list. The output adds the fields outreach actually needs: public email addresses found on the business's site, the contact page URL, and any additional numbers. Not every business publishes an email — in our own smoke tests the share varies a lot by trade, which is exactly why per-lead pricing (pay only when a contact is found) beats per-row pricing.

5. Verify, segment, and load

Before the list goes anywhere near a campaign, run the basics: syntax-check the emails, drop role addresses if your campaign wants named contacts, and segment by the fields you already have — rating band, review count, neighbourhood. Then load the list into your CRM or outreach tool with the source query and extraction date attached to every row, so a list can be refreshed instead of rebuilt.

How much does Maps lead generation cost?

There are three cost shapes in this market, and they behave very differently:

  • Subscriptions to static databases. You pay monthly whether or not you use the data, and the data ages from the day it was compiled. Fine for broad, slow-moving markets; poor for "every X in this city, this week".
  • Per-row scraping tools. You pay for every record extracted, including the ones with no contact details. Simple to understand, quietly expensive at scale, because a large share of raw rows never become leads.
  • Per-result pricing with a contact premium. Places are cheap; the premium is charged only on records that carry a usable public contact. This aligns the bill with the outcome — a list of 1,000 places where 400 yield contacts costs the place price for all 1,000 and the lead price for 400.

With the third model, the arithmetic for a typical local campaign is small: 1,000 places at $0.004 is $4, and 400 verified-contact leads at $0.10 is $40 — a $44 list that you built for one trade in one city, extracted fresh, instead of a rented slice of a national database. Your mileage will vary with the trade's email-publishing habits, which is the honest variable in every Maps campaign.

Quality rules that separate a list from a liability

Freshness beats size. A list extracted this week from live listings will outperform a list three times its size compiled last year, because businesses close, move and change numbers continuously. Prefer tools that pull live at run time over tools that resell a stored index.

Keep the closed ones visible. Extract the status field and filter deliberately. Silently dropping closed businesses at extraction time sounds helpful until you need to know why a territory's count changed.

Deduplicate on identity, not name. The same business appears as "Smith & Sons Plumbing", "Smith and Sons Plumbing Ltd" and a second pin at its yard address. Name-plus-address (or coordinates) matching catches what name matching misses.

Respect the source. The contact details discussed here are ones businesses publish publicly for customers. Use them for relevant business outreach, honour opt-outs immediately, and follow the anti-spam law of the jurisdiction you are sending into. A clean list used badly is still spam; a clean list used precisely is pipeline.

When Maps is the wrong source

Maps lead generation is built for local, listed, customer-facing businesses. It is the wrong tool for finding individual professionals who do not run a listed premises, for businesses that deliberately stay unlisted, and for contact data about private individuals — none of that is on a Maps listing, and none of it should be scraped from anywhere else either. It is also the wrong tool when your market is defined by something Maps cannot see, such as company size or technology stack; for that you want company-register data (for the UK, see our guide to Companies House data) layered on top of the Maps list.

A worked example: one trade, one metro, real arithmetic

Abstract advice is cheap, so here is the whole workflow priced out on a concrete — illustrative — campaign. Suppose you sell a booking-and-reminders service to roofing companies and you want every roofer in one mid-size metro area.

You run three adjacent searches ("roofing contractor", "roof repair", "roofing company") across the metro's main districts and merge the results. Say the raw extraction returns 640 place records. At $0.004 per place, that step costs $2.56. Deduplication on name plus address removes the overlaps between the three phrasings — suppose 90 records go, leaving 550 distinct businesses. Filtering drops the permanently closed (say 25) and the clearly out-of-trade listings the broad phrasings caught (say 15). You now hold 510 live, distinct roofers: the complete visible market, enumerated for less than the cost of a coffee.

Enrichment runs against those 510. Roofing is a phone-first trade, so suppose the yield resembles our home-services smoke test: most records return contact details, roughly four in ten return a public email. The lead charge — $0.10, applied only where a usable public contact was found — lands on perhaps 440 records: $44. The email subset, around 200 addresses, costs nothing extra; it is a property of the records, not a separate product. Total data cost for the campaign: under $47, for a list that did not exist last week, built from live listings, with every record carrying its rating, review count, status and source query.

Now segment it the way a salesperson would. The 60-odd roofers with 100+ reviews are established businesses — pitch efficiency and protection. The 180 with a handful of reviews are growth-stage — pitch visibility. The ones with no website at all, visible instantly in the data, are a separate campaign with a separate first offer. That segmentation took minutes, because the fields were already in the records. This is the part static databases cannot reproduce: not the rows, but the current truth about the rows, in a shape your campaign can act on the same day.

The mistakes that quietly ruin Maps campaigns

  • Buying the biggest list instead of the freshest. Size is the easiest number to sell and the least useful number to buy. A 5,000-row list of mixed age will underperform a 500-row list extracted this week, because your team burns its first calls on closed and moved businesses and loses faith in the rest.
  • Enriching before filtering. Every closed business, duplicate and out-of-territory row you enrich is money spent to make your list worse. The filter step is free; do it first, every time.
  • One search per market. A single query under-enumerates dense markets because of result caps. Adjacent phrasings plus deduplication is not a hack; it is the correct way to enumerate a market through a capped interface.
  • Treating email as the only channel. In phone-first trades, insisting on email coverage shrinks your addressable list to a minority of the market. Use the phone numbers — they are on the listing, they are complete, and the businesses publish them to be called.
  • No provenance, no refresh. A list without source queries and extraction dates cannot be refreshed, only replaced — and a team that cannot refresh will quietly keep using a rotting list. Attach the metadata at build time; it costs nothing and saves the campaign later.
  • Skipping the status field. Permanently-closed flags exist in the data precisely so you never pitch a dead business. Extract the field, filter on it, and keep the closed records in a separate tab rather than deleting them — territory counts over time are market intelligence.

How Maps data fits the rest of your stack

A Maps list is rarely the final dataset; it is the local layer of one. The register layer (for the UK, Companies House) adds legal identity, age, officers and control — the difference between "a dental practice in Leeds" and "a company incorporated in 2019, two directors, active status". The hiring layer (live ATS data, covered in our ATS guide) adds growth signals for the businesses large enough to hire through an ATS. Layered together, the three sources answer the questions a good prospecting operation actually asks: who exists here, are they real and trading, are they growing, and how do I reach them? Maps is the layer that answers the first and last of those, which is why it is usually where the stack starts.

FAQ

Is it legal to extract lead data from Google Maps?

Extracting publicly displayed business information and publicly published contact details is a widely used practice, but the legal position depends on your jurisdiction, the terms of the services involved and how you use the data. This guide is not legal advice. What we can say is how our Actors behave: they collect business listing data and contact details that businesses publish on their own websites, and we recommend using any list in line with the anti-spam and privacy law where you operate, with immediate opt-out handling.

Does Google Maps itself provide email addresses?

No. Listings show names, categories, addresses, phone numbers, websites, ratings and hours — not emails. Email addresses come from the second step: visiting the business's own website, where the business has published them for customers.

How accurate is the data?

It is exactly as accurate as the live listing and the business's website at the moment of the run, because the extraction pulls from the source at run time rather than from a stored index. Businesses do change their details, so refresh lists before major campaigns instead of treating any list as permanent.

Why charge the lead premium only when a contact is found?

Because a place with no public contact is not a lead. Per-row pricing charges you for the failures; per-lead pricing means the premium part of the bill only ever attaches to records you can actually use. See our article on pay-per-result pricing for the full reasoning.

Can I run this for several cities or trades at once?

Yes — run one search per trade-and-city combination and merge the outputs, deduplicating on name plus address. Overlapping phrasings of the same search are also a legitimate way to widen coverage of a single market.

What format does the output come in?

A structured dataset (JSON, with CSV/Excel export from the Apify console) — one record per place, with the listing fields and, where found, the contact fields. It loads directly into spreadsheets, CRMs and outreach tools.
