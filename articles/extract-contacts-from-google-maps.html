How to Extract Contacts from Google Maps (and What Actually Works)

Google Maps listings do not contain email addresses — so where do "Maps leads with emails" come from? This article explains the real two-step process, the failure modes at each step, and how to judge any tool that sells you the result.

The misconception to clear up first

Search for "extract contacts from Google Maps" and you will find tools promising names, phone numbers and emails "from Google Maps". Only part of that promise lives on Maps. A business listing shows the business name, category, address, phone number, website, rating, review count and opening hours. It has never shown an email address. When a tool delivers emails alongside Maps data, the emails were collected in a second step: the tool followed the listing's website link and extracted the contact details the business publishes on its own site.

This is not a pedantic distinction. It determines what can go wrong, what the data costs, and what you may legitimately do with it. The listing data is Google's presentation of facts about a business. The email is the business's own publication, on its own website, placed there so customers can get in touch. Any honest vendor in this space should be able to explain which step produced which field — and if they cannot, assume the data came from a stored database of unknown age instead.

Step one: extracting the listing data

The first step is a search. Google Maps answers natural-language queries — "dentist in Berlin", "plumber near Leeds", "HVAC contractor Austin" — with a scrollable panel of matching businesses. An extractor works through that panel and records, for every business, the structured fields the listing carries.

Three practical realities shape this step:

  • Result caps. A single search returns a limited panel of results. For dense markets — "restaurant in London" — one query will not enumerate the whole market. The standard technique is to split the territory: search by neighbourhood, by sub-category ("Italian restaurant", "sushi restaurant"), or by adjacent phrasings, then merge and deduplicate. Any tool that claims a complete city-wide list from one query is overselling.
  • Duplicates are structural. The same business can appear under name variants, at a head-office pin and a branch pin, or in several overlapping searches. Deduplication on name plus address — or on coordinates — is not optional polish; it is the difference between a list and a pile.
  • The website field is the payload. For contact extraction, the single most important field in the listing is the website URL, because it is the bridge to step two. Records without a website can still yield a phone number, but they will never yield an email, and a good extractor should tell you the website coverage of a run before you pay for enrichment.

Step two: from website to contact details

Given a business website, contact extraction is a small research task repeated at scale:

  1. Fetch the homepage and the obvious contact pages. The high-yield targets are the homepage footer, the contact page, the imprint or legal page (in several European countries the imprint is legally required and almost always carries an email), and any "about" or "team" page.
  2. Extract and classify what is found. Email addresses, additional phone numbers, and contact-form URLs. Classification matters: an info@ address is a business contact; a webmaster@ or a newsletter-vendor address is not a lead. A careful extractor prefers addresses published in a customer-facing context.
  3. Unwrap the indirections. Real websites hide behind redirect chains and link shorteners, and the listing's website field itself sometimes contains a redirect rather than the final address. (We hit exactly this in our own Maps engine — listings intermittently returned a redirect wrapper instead of the real website — and fixed it by unwrapping redirects before the fetch. It is a small detail that silently kills email yield if you miss it.)
  4. Record provenance. Every extracted email should carry the URL it was found on. Provenance is what lets you audit a list, defend it, and refresh it — and its absence is a reliable sign of a resold database.

Why the yield varies so much by trade

Not every business publishes an email, and the publishing habit is a property of the trade, not of the tool. In our own smoke tests: a five-term dental pack run against Berlin practices returned contact details for 93% of places and an email for 87% — dental practices run appointment businesses and publish contact channels as a matter of course. A six-trade home-services pack returned contacts for 87% of places but emails for only 40% — many sole traders publish a mobile number and a contact form, and no email at all. Neither result says the tool is good or bad; both say something true about how those trades prefer to be contacted. Judge any vendor's yield claims against the trade, and be suspicious of a single headline percentage applied to all markets.

The failure modes nobody advertises

Contact forms instead of addresses. A growing share of small-business sites offer only a form. There is no email to extract, and any tool that "finds" one for such a business invented it or bought it from somewhere you do not want to know about. The correct output is an empty email field and a contact-page URL.

Stale websites. The listing may point at a domain that has lapsed, been parked, or been replaced by a directory page. Extractors should detect parked domains and report the record as no-contact rather than harvesting the parking page's addresses.

Chain and franchise structures. A listing for a franchised location often links to the national chain's site, whose published email belongs to head office, not the branch. Whether that is a defect depends on who you sell to — but the record should make the structure visible rather than presenting a head-office inbox as a branch contact.

Role accounts and third parties. Sites publish addresses for their web designer, their booking platform and their newsletter provider. Indiscriminate extraction fills your list with people who have never heard of the business you are trying to reach. Context filtering — preferring addresses on contact and imprint pages over addresses buried in blog comments or policy text — is where careful tools earn their price.

How pricing should work for this — and how to spot the bad version

Step one is cheap and step two is not: fetching and parsing arbitrary websites costs real compute, and a large share of fetches find nothing publishable. That cost shape creates two honest pricing models and one dishonest one.

The dishonest model charges per row for "leads with emails" and quietly delivers rows without emails, or pads the list from a stored database. The honest models are: charge per place plus a premium only when a contact is actually found (our model: $0.004 per place, $0.10 only when the record carries a usable public contact), or charge per fetch attempt with the yield risk on you. Between the honest two, per-contact pricing is the one where the vendor eats the cost of the no-email majority — which is also the strongest signal that the vendor trusts its own extraction. For a one-off list of fifty businesses, do it manually: search Maps, open the sites, copy the details into a spreadsheet. You will finish in an afternoon and learn your market's real yield in the process. Anatomy of a contact hunt: where addresses actually hide

To judge extraction quality, it helps to know where a small business's email address physically lives on its website. In rough order of yield:

  • The imprint or legal page. In Germany and several other European jurisdictions, businesses are legally required to publish an imprint with contact details. For those markets this page is close to a guaranteed find, and it is a large part of why the Berlin dental smoke test returned emails for 87% of practices. A serious extractor checks the imprint early; a lazy one never finds it, because the link is usually a single quiet word in the footer.
  • The contact page. The obvious target, and genuinely productive — but read it properly. Many contact pages lead with a form and place the email below it, or on a "for emergencies" line, or inside the directions paragraph. Extraction that stops at "this page has a form" misses the address sitting under the form.
  • The homepage footer. Especially on older small-business sites, the footer carries the full contact block: address, phone, email. Footers are also where the web designer's own email sometimes sits — context filtering is what keeps the designer out of your list.
  • mailto: links. An address used as a link target exists in the page markup even when the visible text says only "Email us". Extractors that read rendered text alone miss these; extractors that read the markup catch them. The reverse failure also exists: addresses that appear in markup as spam-trap decoys or in commented-out code from a previous design. Mature pipelines weigh where in the page an address was found, not merely that it was found.
  • Obfuscated addresses. Some sites write "info [at] example.com" or render the address as an image to deter harvesters. Whether to de-obfuscate is a policy question, not a technical one: the business published the address for human customers while signalling it does not want bulk collection. Our position is that the honest output respects the signal — record the contact page, leave the email field empty. A vendor that boasts about defeating obfuscation is telling you something about how it will treat your instructions too.

The one thing you must never do: guess addresses

There is a technique this article has deliberately not described as an option: constructing addresses from patterns — first name, last name, common formats — for people at a business. Pattern-guessed addresses are not extracted data; they are fabrications with a plausible shape. They bounce, they hit the wrong people, they burn sender reputation, and when they do reach someone, the recipient knows instantly that they were never a published contact. Any vendor whose email yield for a phone-first trade looks impossibly high is either guessing, reselling a database, or both. The yield numbers in this article — 87% for Berlin dentists, 40% for home services — are what honest extraction looks like: high where businesses publish, modest where they do not. If a competitor quotes you a flat 95% across all trades, you now know which questions to ask.

A five-minute audit for any vendor's sample

Before buying contact data from anyone, including us, run this audit on a sample of twenty records. Take five records that carry emails and visit the businesses' websites yourself: can you find each address, on a public page, within a minute? Take five records with no email and check the sites: is the absence real — form-only, parked, obfuscated — or did the extractor simply not look at the imprint page? Check two records against the live Maps listing: do name, address and phone match what Google shows today? Finally, check the sample for pattern-guessed addresses by picking any email with a personal name in it and looking for that person on the business's site. Twenty records and fifteen minutes will tell you more about a vendor's pipeline than any sales page — ours included, which is why every Actor page on this site carries a real sample record for exactly this purpose.

Inside the pipeline: how extraction actually processes a website

For buyers who want to judge a vendor's engineering rather than its copywriting, here is the sequence a serious contact-extraction pipeline runs for each place record — and the decision points where cheap implementations quietly lose yield.

Domain normalisation comes first. The listing's website field is messy in the wild: www and non-www variants, trailing paths pointing at a specific landing page, http beside https, occasionally a social-media profile instead of a site. The pipeline canonicalises to the site's root, resolves redirects to the final destination, and unwraps redirect wrappers (including the ones the listing itself sometimes carries — the bug we fixed in our own engine, where a wrapped URL silently zeroed a record's email yield). A pipeline that fetches the raw field as-is will fail on a meaningful share of records before extraction even begins.

Fetch strategy is a yield decision. Most small-business sites are static and fetch cleanly. Some render their contact details with JavaScript; some sit behind bot checks that a datacenter fetch trips over. A pipeline has to decide, per site, whether the plain fetch sufficed — the page contains a footer, a contact link, recognisable structure — or whether the page it received is a shell. Treating a shell as "no contact found" is the single largest silent yield leak in this industry, and it never appears in any vendor's marketing; it appears only in the audit described above, as records whose "missing" emails are visible on the site to any human who visits.

Localisation decides which pages exist. The high-value pages have different names in different markets: Contact, Kontakt, Impressum, Contacto, Contatti, Mentions légales. A pipeline built for one language's web will systematically underperform in every other market, because it never visits the page where the address lives. This is a second, quieter reason our Berlin dental yield (87%) is worth trusting as a pipeline signal: reaching it required the German pages to be first-class citizens of the crawl, not afterthoughts.

Politeness is also engineering. A business's website is its shopfront, not a bulk-data endpoint. Sensible pipelines fetch a handful of pages per site, cache within the run so a chain's shared pages are fetched once rather than per branch, and never hammer a small site with parallel requests. This is not only ethics; a pipeline that gets IP-blocked by half the small-business web has a yield problem of its own making. When you evaluate vendors, "how many pages do you fetch per business, and how do you treat a site that says no" is a technical question with technical answers — ask it.

None of this is visible in the output dataset, which is precisely why the audit matters: the dataset shows you the yield, and the yield is the sum of these decisions. Two vendors can run the same listings through the same nominal process and deliver email coverage ten points apart, and the difference is entirely in the unglamorous steps above.

FAQ

Can I get email addresses directly from Google Maps?

No. Maps listings do not contain email addresses. Emails attached to Maps lead lists come from the businesses' own websites, reached via the listing's website field. Any source that says otherwise is misdescribing its process.

What share of businesses will have an email?

It depends on the trade and the market, not on the tool. In our smoke tests, Berlin dental practices yielded emails for 87% of places, while a home-services pack yielded 40%. Expect phone coverage to be much higher than email coverage almost everywhere.

Are extracted emails verified?

Extraction establishes that an address was published on the business's site at run time; it does not guarantee the mailbox is monitored or that a given message will be accepted. Run your own validation pass before a large send, and always honour opt-outs immediately.

Why do some records have a website but no email?

Because the business publishes only a contact form, or publishes its email as an image, or the site is parked or unreachable. The honest output for such a record is the contact-page URL and an empty email field.

Is this the business's private data?

No — the process described here collects only details the business itself publishes for customers: listing facts and contact details on its public website. It does not touch private individuals' data, and we recommend using any resulting list under the anti-spam and privacy law of the jurisdiction you send into.

Can I extract contacts for several trades at once?

Run one search per trade and location, then merge and deduplicate on name plus address. Pre-configured packs for home services and dental practices exist because those two trades are requested most.
