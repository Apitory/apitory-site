Building a Niche Job Board on Live Data (Without Becoming a Scraping Company)

A niche job board lives or dies on two things: completeness inside its niche, and never sending an applicant to a closed role. Both are data problems, and both are solved better by pulling live from applicant tracking systems than by crawling the web. Here is the architecture, the economics and the operating routine.

Why niche boards win, and why most of them die

The generalist job boards own breadth. What they cannot own is depth in a single profession, technology, region or work style — and depth is what applicants and employers in a niche actually want. A board that lists every remote data-engineering role, or every nursing vacancy in one state, or every job at companies using one technology stack, gives its audience a reason to bookmark it that a generalist never will.

Most niche boards do not die from lack of audience. They die from data operations. The founder starts by collecting listings by hand, then writes scrapers, then maintains scrapers — and within a year the business is a scraping company with a job board attached. Career pages change their markup, JavaScript rendering hides the listings, IP blocks arrive, and every hour spent on parser maintenance is an hour not spent on the audience. The second killer is quieter: dead listings. An applicant who clicks through to a role that closed two weeks ago does not come back, and an employer whose filled role keeps attracting applicants stops posting. A niche board's entire asset is trust, and stale inventory spends it.

Both failure modes come from the same decision: treating job listings as web pages to be harvested instead of as records in systems to be queried. This article is about the other architecture.

The insight: your niche is a company list

A niche is usually defined by a set of employers. "Remote data-engineering roles" means the openings at companies that hire data engineers remotely. "Jobs in climate tech" means the openings at a few hundred identifiable companies. Whatever the niche, there is a list of companies whose hiring defines it — and those companies run their hiring through applicant tracking systems (ATS) such as Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, SmartRecruiters and Workable.

Those systems publish the company's current openings as structured data, because the company's own careers page is built on the same feed. If your board pulls from the feeds, three of the classic problems simply disappear:

  • No parsing. Title, department, location, workplace type, description and application URL arrive as fields, not as HTML to be reverse-engineered per employer.
  • No staleness. A role appears in your inventory when the employer opens it and disappears when the employer closes it, because your pull and their careers page read the same source. Dead listings — the trust killer — are structurally impossible rather than merely cleaned up after.
  • No per-employer maintenance. A company redesigning its website changes nothing for you. The feed contract is with the ATS platform, and it changes rarely and uniformly across all its customers at once.

What remains is the work that actually differentiates a board: choosing the company list well, classifying the openings into your niche's taxonomy, and building the audience.

The architecture, concretely

The data layer of a live-fed niche board has four moving parts:

  1. The company list. Your niche's employers, each identified by the ATS board token or career-site identifier the pull needs. Building this list is editorial work, done once and then maintained: companies enter the niche, get acquired, or switch ATS platforms, and a quarterly review keeps the list honest. Expect the initial list to be the single highest-leverage afternoon you spend on the whole board.
  2. The scheduled pull. A job-data Actor — ours is Career Site Jobs — Live ATS API — takes the company list, queries the five ATS platforms' public feeds at run time, and returns one structured dataset of everything those companies have open right now. Daily is the right cadence for most boards; high-velocity niches can pull twice a day without any change in architecture.
  3. The diff and classify step. Compare today's dataset with yesterday's on job identity. New records are new postings — publish them, and they are also your freshest marketing material ("14 new roles this week"). Vanished records are closures — unpublish immediately. Then classify each record into your board's taxonomy using the structured fields the feed gives you: department for function, location and workplace type for geography and remote status, and your own keyword rules over the title for specialisations.
  4. The board itself. A static or database-backed listing site whose only data source is the classified dataset. Because the dataset is structured and complete, the board can offer the filters applicants actually use — location, remote, department, seniority keywords — without any of them depending on scraped free text.

Nothing in this architecture requires you to operate a browser farm, rotate proxies, or maintain a parser per employer. The crawling problem has been moved to the one party who does not have it: the ATS platform itself.

The economics

Live pulls are priced per delivered result. Halve the cadence and you halve the bill; the inventory is still fresher than any weekly crawl. Compare that with the real cost of the scraping route — not the proxy bill, which is the small part, but the maintenance hours, which are the part that quietly eats a founder's week every time a major employer redesigns its careers page.

The per-result shape also matches how a young board grows. When your niche list is 80 companies, you pay for 80 companies' worth of openings. The data bill scales with inventory, not with your ambition, and there is no subscription floor charging you for a database you are not using. What live data does not solve

Honesty about the boundaries matters more than enthusiasm:

  • Employers outside the ATS universe. Companies that hire through their own portals, through agencies, or only via generalist boards will not appear in ATS feeds. For most professional niches the ATS coverage is the market's centre of gravity, but a board that promises completeness should know exactly which named employers it cannot see and say so.
  • Discovery. A feed pull answers "what do these companies have open?" It does not answer "which companies exist in this niche?" The company list is your editorial product; growing it is research, not engineering.
  • Enrichment beyond the posting. Salary bands, tech-stack extraction and seniority inference are classification work on top of the record. The feed gives you the employer's own text and fields; anything beyond that is your layer to build — which is fine, because that layer is where a niche board's taxonomy advantage lives.

An operating routine that keeps the board trustworthy

Daily: pull, diff, publish new, unpublish closed. Automate it entirely; a human should only see failures.

Weekly: publish the change summary — new roles by company, notable closures. This is content your audience cannot get from a generalist board, and it is generated by the diff you already compute.

Monthly: audit the company list against your niche: new entrants, acquisitions, ATS migrations. Spot-check ten records against the employers' own careers pages; because both read the same feed, they should match exactly, and any mismatch is a configuration bug worth finding early.

Quarterly: review classification quality — are roles landing in the right categories, are your keyword rules ageing? The taxonomy is the product; the feed is plumbing.

A board run this way spends its founder's hours on audience and taxonomy, which are the things a generalist cannot copy, instead of on parsers, which are the thing nobody should have to copy at all.

Monetisation: what a live-fed board can charge for

A board whose inventory is complete-in-niche and never stale has four realistic revenue shapes, and the data architecture supports all of them. Employer products are the classic: featured listings, employer pages, and distribution — but note the inversion the feed creates. On a scraped board, employers pay for presence; on a live-fed board their listings already arrive automatically, so what you sell them is prominence and audience, which is a healthier product because it does not depend on holding their own data hostage. Applicant products — alerts, early access, application tools — monetise the audience the freshness attracts; the weekly diff ("new roles at these companies") is a genuinely valuable alert precisely because it is generated from real changes. Data products are the quiet third leg: the same classified dataset, sold as a feed to recruiters and analysts who want the niche's hiring picture without running a board. Affiliate and referral arrangements with the ATS platforms and adjacent services fit naturally, because your board already sits on top of their ecosystem. The common thread: none of these require the board to win a breadth war with the generalists. Each monetises depth, freshness and trust — the three properties the live-data architecture produces as by-products.

How boards that scrape actually die: a failure anatomy

It is worth being concrete about the alternative, because the scraping route looks cheaper at every single step and loses only in total. Month one: the founder writes parsers for the fifty employers that matter most, over a weekend, and feels clever. Month three: the list is two hundred employers, a third of the parsers have silently degraded — a redesign here, a JavaScript wall there — and the board's inventory quietly fills with duplicates and dead roles that nobody has time to audit. Month six: the largest employer in the niche blocks the crawler's IP range; the board's flagship coverage vanishes overnight, and the founder spends a week on proxies and rendering infrastructure instead of the audience. Month nine: an applicant writes the review that matters — "half the jobs here are closed" — and it is true, and it ranks. None of these failures are caused by incompetence; they are the operating cost of treating records as web pages. The feed architecture's real product is not the data. It is the absence of that entire failure class, purchased for about $3 per thousand records — which is why we think the interesting question for a prospective board founder is not "can I scrape this cheaper?" but "which business am I actually trying to be in: the niche, or the scraping?"

Your first two weeks, in order

  1. Days 1–2: the company list. Draft your niche's employers from your own knowledge, association directories and competitor boards' customer logos. Identify each one's ATS and board token. Two hundred well-chosen companies beat two thousand scraped guesses.
  2. Day 3: the first pull. Run the full list once through Career Site Jobs — Live ATS API. Expect a few thousand records for a healthy niche; the run costs a few dollars and tells you your true inventory for the first time.
  3. Days 4–5: the taxonomy. Build classification rules over department, location, workplace type and title keywords. Manually review a hundred records against your rules; fix the rules, not the records.
  4. Week 2: the board and the diff. Publish the classified inventory, switch on the daily pull and the diff, and start the weekly change summary from the first diff you compute. Your launch announcement writes itself: "Every open role at these companies, updated daily, nothing listed that has closed."

SEO and the audience flywheel — without becoming a spam farm

A niche board's listings are naturally search-shaped: "remote data engineer jobs", "nursing jobs in Ohio", "climate tech careers" are queries with intent, and a board that genuinely holds the niche's complete, current inventory has the honest claim to rank for them. The temptation to resist is programmatic inflation — generating thousands of thin pages for every conceivable query permutation. That strategy borrows against the same trust the board exists to hold, and search engines have become competent at foreclosing on it. The durable version is narrower and stronger: one page per real listing (real because it exists, current because the feed keeps it so, removed when the role closes), category pages that aggregate genuine inventory with genuine counts, and company pages that show a real employer's real openings. A page that says "14 open roles" and lists 14 open roles is a better search asset than a page that says "thousands of jobs" and lists a database's memory of them — and it converts better, because the applicant who clicks through finds the promise kept. The flywheel then turns on its own: freshness earns rankings, rankings bring applicants, applicants justify employer products, and employer revenue funds the company-list research that widens coverage. Every part of that loop depends on the inventory being true, which is the entire argument of this article restated as a growth strategy.

One more compounding asset deserves mention: the archive. Because your pulls are timestamped datasets, you accumulate the niche's hiring history as a by-product — which companies hired through which quarters, how the niche's demand moved, which skills' mentions rose. A year in, that archive is a content and data asset no new competitor can copy quickly, because it cannot be backfilled: the past states of the feeds are gone for anyone who was not collecting them. Boards rarely plan for the archive on day one; the ones that keep their raw datasets from the first pull are quietly building the moat.

Choosing the niche: a scoring pass before you build

Not every niche deserves a board, and the live-data model makes the test cheap enough to run before committing. Score a candidate niche on five questions. Is the employer set enumerable? You should be able to draft a hundred core companies from knowledge and directories in a day; a niche whose employers cannot be listed cannot be feed-fed. Do those employers hire through ATS platforms? Run a sample of thirty against the Actor: if most return live openings, the feed covers your market; if most return nothing because they hire elsewhere, the architecture does not fit this niche. Is hiring velocity sufficient? A niche with forty open roles at any moment cannot fill a board; a few hundred is a working inventory. The same sample run measures this for a few dollars. Is the audience identifiable and reachable? A profession with associations, communities and its own vocabulary is marketable; a diffuse category is not. Do the generalists serve it badly? They almost always do — but check whether their failure is inventory (fixable by you) or audience habit (harder). A niche that passes all five is a build decision, and the total cost of finding out was one sample run and a day of list work — which is, in miniature, the argument for the whole approach: decisions priced in dollars and days, not in quarters of scraping infrastructure.

FAQ

How fresh will my board's listings be?

As fresh as your pull cadence. The Actor queries the ATS feeds at run time, and the feeds are what the employers' own careers pages display. A daily pull means no listing on your board is more than a day behind the employer's own site, and closures disappear at the next pull.

Which ATS platforms can I pull from?

Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, SmartRecruiters and Workable through the multi-system Actor; a Greenhouse-only Actor exists if your niche lives entirely there.

What does it cost to run a board on this?

$3.00 per 1,000 job records delivered. A 400-company niche with about 2,000 open roles costs roughly $6 per daily pull. You pay nothing for companies with no openings beyond the run's start event.

Can the feed tell me when a specific company starts hiring?

Yes — that is the diff. A company that had no open roles and now has three produces three new records in the next pull. The same mechanism powers hiring-signal products for recruiters and sales teams.

What if an employer in my niche does not use a supported ATS?

Then its openings are not in the feed, and no amount of configuration changes that. Cover the employers you can, name the gap honestly if completeness matters to your positioning, and add such employers through their own channels if they are individually important.

Do I need to handle scraping, proxies or rendering?

No. The pull is an API query against the ATS platforms' public feeds. There is no HTML parsing, no browser rendering and no per-employer maintenance in your stack.
