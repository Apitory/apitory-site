Decoding the UK Companies House Register: A Field Guide for Risk, Sales and Enrichment Teams

TL;DR: Companies House is the UK's free, authoritative register of company facts — profile, officers, persons with significant control, filings — and a full record costs $0.001 per company through our Actor. This guide shows what's in a record, what it can't tell you, and how to run it at scale without breaking the public API.

The register, in one paragraph

Every limited company in the United Kingdom is incorporated through Companies House, the government's registrar, and the core facts of its existence are public record: its name and number, its registered office address, its status, its incorporation date, its industry codes, its directors (officers), the people with significant control over it, and a public filing history of the documents it has submitted over its life. The register exists so that anyone doing business with a company can check what that company is and who stands behind it.

For data buyers, this makes Companies House unusual in two ways. First, it is authoritative: this is not information inferred from websites or bought from brokers; it is the statutory record, filed by the companies themselves under legal obligation. Second, it is open: the data is published for reuse, which is why an ecosystem of products — due-diligence tools, lead-generation services, KYB (know-your-business) checks, market researchers — is built on it. Our UK Companies House Data Actor is one such product: it returns a full company record for $1.00 per 1,000 records — that is $0.001 per company, composed of $0.0007 for the record and $0.0003 for the officers and PSC data that complete it.

Anatomy of a full company record

Four parts make up a complete record, and assembled together they answer the questions business data is usually bought to answer: does this company exist, is it still trading, who runs it, who owns it, and how long has it been around?

  • Company profile — registered name, company number, status (active, dissolved, in liquidation and so on), incorporation date, company type, registered office address, and SIC codes, the standard industrial classification codes that state what the company says it does.
  • Officers — current and former directors and secretaries, with appointment and resignation dates and the details the register publishes about them. In our own verification, a full record for a large listed company (Tesco PLC) returned 75 officers — the register's history runs deep for long-lived companies.
  • Persons with significant control (PSC) — the individuals or entities that own or control the company, the closest thing UK data has to a beneficial-ownership record, and the field that KYB and compliance buyers care about most.
  • Filing and accounts signals — the filing history shows what has been submitted and when: accounts dates, confirmation statements, charges, which supports everything from credit screening to "recently incorporated" targeting.

Where this data earns its keep

KYB and onboarding checks. When a business signs up a business customer, someone has to verify that the customer is a real, active company and that the person signing is connected to it. A full Companies House record — profile plus officers plus PSC — is the standard first pass for UK counterparties: cheap enough to run on every signup, authoritative enough to anchor the file.

Lead generation and market mapping. Incorporation date plus SIC code plus registered office geography is a targeting engine: every company incorporated in the last 90 days in these industry codes in this region is a list a salesperson can work, and nobody had to scrape it, because the register publishes it. The same structure supports market sizing — how many active companies exist in a sector, in a city, of a given age. For teams layering operational data on top, our guide to Google Maps lead generation covers the trading-location and contact side the register doesn't.

Due diligence and risk screening. Status changes, officer churn, overdue filings and control changes are all risk signals, and they are all in the public record with dates attached. Screening a supplier or partner list against the register on a schedule turns those signals into an early-warning feed.

Data enrichment. If you hold a list of company names — customers, prospects, portfolio companies — the register is the reference to resolve them against: match the name, attach the company number, and every subsequent update becomes a lookup instead of a research task.

What the register will not tell you

The register records what companies are required to file, and its limits follow from that. It does not contain revenue figures for most companies (small companies file abbreviated accounts), employee counts you can rely on, trading addresses that differ from the registered office, or contact details for outreach — the registered office is a legal address, frequently an accountant's office, and treating it as a sales address is a category error. It also cannot tell you whether a company is good at what it does; an active status means the company exists and files, nothing more.

That is why register data is usually one layer in a stack. The register establishes identity, status, ownership and control. Operational data — where the business actually trades, how customers rate it, how to reach it — comes from sources like Google Maps listings, which the lead-generation guide linked above covers in detail; the two combine well: the register tells you a company is real and who controls it, the listing tells you where it trades and how to contact it.

Reading a record like a practitioner

Suppose you are onboarding a new UK supplier and you pull its full record. Status first: "active" is the baseline; anything else — dissolution in progress, liquidation, administration — ends the conversation until a human looks. Incorporation date sets your prior: a company formed eleven years ago with continuous filings is a different risk from one formed eleven weeks ago, and neither is automatically bad, but they get different questions. Registered office: note whether it is a plausible trading address or an accountant's or formation agent's address shared by hundreds of companies; the latter is normal for small companies and simply tells you the register will not give you a trading location.

SIC codes confirm the company describes itself as doing what it told you it does; a mismatch is a question, not a verdict, because codes are self-declared and often stale. Officers: check that the person signing your contract actually appears as a current director, and scan the resignation pattern — a board that has turned over twice in a year is a fact you want before you extend terms. PSC: identify who ultimately controls the company; if the PSC is another company, follow the chain one level, because your counterparty's real decision-maker may sit there. Filing history: accounts and confirmation statements filed on time suggest a competently administered company; a trail of overdue filings is one of the cheapest distress signals in business data.

Turning a static lookup into a monitoring feed

A single lookup answers "what is true now". The more valuable pattern for risk and sales teams is the repeated lookup: pull the same portfolio of companies on a schedule and diff the records. The changes are events with business meaning — a status change is a counterparty event; a new director or a PSC change is a control event; a company crossing an age or filing threshold is a targeting event; a wave of new incorporations in a SIC code and region is a market event.

Because register data is cheap per record, a 20,000-company portfolio re-pulled monthly costs about $20 at our per-record price — monitoring economics that would be absurd with brokered data are trivial here. The discipline required is the same as with any diff: match on company number, stamp every pull with its date, and store the previous record rather than overwriting it, because the history of a company's register entries is often worth more than the latest snapshot.

Register data versus commercial company databases

Commercial databases absolutely have their place: they add estimated financials, employee modelling, group structures across borders, and contact data the register does not hold. The buying mistake is paying database prices for register facts. If the question is "does this UK company exist, is it active, who directs it, who controls it, when was it incorporated and what does it file" — that is the register, it is public, and its fair price at scale is a tenth of a cent per company, not a four-figure annual licence. Pay the database for the layer it actually adds; pull the register layer from the register. The same verifiability standard applies in both directions: whatever the source, test it with three companies you already know, and compare what comes back against ground truth before you build on it.

Why names lie and numbers don't

Every workflow in this guide eventually meets the register's oldest trap: company names are unreliable identifiers. Names repeat — nothing stops two unrelated companies from carrying near-identical names in different regions. Names change, with the company number persisting underneath. Dissolved companies' names return to the pool and are reused by new companies with no connection to the original. Trading names ("trading as") may never appear on the register at all.

The operational rules follow directly. Resolve a name to a company number once, manually if the match matters, and store the number as the identity for everything after. When matching in bulk, score candidates on more than the name string — registered office postcode, incorporation date and officer surnames all discriminate between same-name companies — and route low-confidence matches to a human rather than letting them flow silently into a KYB file. When a search returns a dissolved company with the right name, do not stop: the live successor may be the next result. These are small disciplines, but they are the difference between register data as a reliable system of reference and register data as a source of confident errors — the register itself is exact; it is the joinery that fails, and the joinery is yours.

Working the incorporation feed for new-business outreach

One of the register's most commercially used patterns deserves a fuller description, because it inverts the usual data-buying direction. Most business data is bought about companies that already exist in your world — your customers, your prospects, your portfolio. Incorporation data is about companies that entered the world this week. Every new registration is a business at the moment of maximum need: it will open a bank account, choose accounting, register for taxes, build a website, and buy a dozen services in its first ninety days, and the register publishes its arrival — name, number, SIC codes, registered office, first officers — as a matter of public record.

Sellers of formation-adjacent services run standing queries against the register's new-company flow, segmented by industry code and geography, and work the results while incumbents' databases are still waiting for their next compilation cycle. The discipline that makes the pattern work is speed plus restraint: the list is small, fresh and genuinely in-market, which rewards a precise, relevant approach and punishes blasting — a founder who receives forty identical pitches in week one learns to ignore the channel, and the vendors who taught them that lesson have taxed everyone else's future outreach. Pulled per record at register prices, a weekly new-company pull for a defined SIC-and-region segment costs very little; the scarce input is the follow-up quality, not the data.

Why pay for a wrapper when the API is free

A fair question deserves a straight answer: Companies House itself offers bulk data products and a free API, so what is a per-record Actor for? The bulk products serve a different job. A full-register snapshot is the right tool for register-wide analytics — sector studies, incorporation trend research — and the wrong tool for "verify this supplier", "enrich these 4,000 prospects" or "re-check my portfolio monthly": each of those would require downloading and maintaining a copy of the entire register to answer questions about a sliver of it.

The API serves the sliver well but leaves the assembly with you: a full record means several calls per company (profile, officers, PSC, filings), plus rate-limit management, plus normalisation into one schema, maintained forever. The Actor is that assembly, productised: one input, one complete record per company, fetched at run time, charged per record at a tenth of a cent. For a team that needs register answers inside a workflow — onboarding, CRM enrichment, portfolio monitoring — the comparison is not $0.001 against free; it is $0.001 against the engineering and maintenance of an integration, repeated every time the register's interface shifts. Buy the bulk product for analytics. Build on the API if register data is your core product. For lookups inside a business process, the wrapped record is the rational shape — and its price makes the decision an easy one to reverse.

Respecting the register as a shared public resource

Companies House data is open, but open is not the same as unlimited. The official API is a shared public resource with rate limits, and products built on it are expected to behave like good citizens: cache what you have fetched rather than re-fetching it in a loop, respect the published limits, and design for lookups rather than bulk hammering. This is one reason the per-record Actor model fits the register well: a buyer asks for the specific companies they need — a signup being verified, a prospect list being enriched — and pays per record delivered, which keeps both the bill and the load on the public API proportional to actual use.

Two quality habits matter when you build on register data. First, match on company number wherever possible: names are duplicated, traded under and changed; the number is the identity. Second, record your fetch date: the register is a living record — officers resign, statuses change — and a record without a date will silently pretend to be current forever. A field you pulled last year is a historical fact, not a current one.

Common questions before you run it

Is Companies House data free? The register is public and the official API is free to use within its rate limits. What you pay a data product for is the fetching, assembly and delivery — turning API responses into complete, structured records at the volume and reliability your workflow needs — priced here at $0.001 per full company record.

What is a PSC? A person with significant control: an individual or entity that owns or controls a company — for example holding more than 25% of shares or voting rights. PSC data is the register's beneficial-ownership layer and a standard input to KYB checks.

Does the record include contact details like email or phone? No. The register publishes legal and structural facts — registered office address, officers, control, filings — not marketing contact details. For trading addresses and public contact channels, pair register data with listing data such as Google Maps.

How current is the data? Our Actor fetches from the official register at run time, so a record reflects the register as it stands when you run it. Companies file changes continuously, so for monitoring use cases, re-pull on a schedule and diff.

Can I check companies in bulk? Yes — supply a list of company numbers (or names to resolve) and each returns a full record, charged per record delivered. Matching on company number is strongly recommended over name matching.

What is a SIC code? The UK Standard Industrial Classification code a company selects at incorporation to describe its activities. Companies can list several, and they are self-declared, so treat SIC as a targeting signal rather than a verified description of current trade.
