Home Services Leads for Agencies: Building Plumber, HVAC and Electrician Lists Your Clients Can Actually Call

Agencies selling to home-services businesses have a list problem: the trades are local, numerous, and badly served by national databases. This guide shows how to build fresh, territory-exact lead lists from Google Maps — and how to package them so clients renew.

The agency problem with home-services data

Home services — plumbing, electrical, HVAC, roofing, cleaning, pest control, landscaping — is one of the best verticals an agency can serve. The businesses spend real money on customers because a single job is worth hundreds or thousands, they are numerous in every metro area, and most of them market badly or not at all. If you sell websites, ads, review management or a CRM to local trades, your constraint is rarely the offer. It is the list.

National lead databases serve this vertical poorly for structural reasons. Their coverage is compiled slowly, so the newest businesses — often the keenest buyers — are missing. Their territory definitions are postal, not practical: a client's real service area is "these suburbs, this side of the river", not a radius around a postcode. And their records carry the fields a database finds easy — name, address, a phone — while missing the fields an agency actually prospects with: does this business have a website at all, what is its rating, how many reviews, and is there a public email?

The alternative is to build the list at the moment you need it, from the source your clients' customers use: Google Maps. Our Google Maps Home Services Leads Actor exists for exactly this workflow — a six-trade pack prefilled for the core home-services trades, priced at $0.004 per place and $0.10 only when a record carries a usable public contact. This guide explains how agencies use it, and the judgements that separate a list clients love from a list they churn on.

What an agency-grade record looks like

A prospecting record for a trades business should let a salesperson qualify the account in ten seconds:

  • Identity: business name, trade category, address, service-area signals.
  • Reachability: phone number, website, and — where the business publishes one — an email address. In our smoke test of the home-services pack, 87% of extracted places carried contact details and 40% carried an email. Plan your outreach mix around numbers like those: phone-first for the majority, email as a second channel, not the only one.
  • Marketing signals: rating and review count are the agency's opening wedge. A 4.9-star plumber with 300 reviews needs a different pitch (protect and extend) from a 4.1-star plumber with 9 reviews (your first sale is reputation itself). A business with no website at all is a different campaign again — and Maps data shows you all three at a glance.
  • Status: trading or permanently closed. Nothing burns a client's trust in your list like their SDR calling a business that shut last year. Live extraction carries the status flag; filter on it.

The build: territory, trades, then enrichment

Start from the client's service area, not from a city name. Translate "we cover the north side" into the actual suburbs and towns, and run one search per trade per locality. Overlapping coverage is fine — deduplication on name plus address is a solved step, and gaps are not recoverable later, while duplicates are.

Run the six trades as six lists, not one. Plumbers and pest-control companies buy differently, churn differently and answer the phone differently. Keeping the trade as a first-class field from extraction onward lets you report, price and pitch per trade instead of handing the client an undifferentiated blob called "home services".

Filter before you enrich. Place extraction is cheap; contact enrichment is where the money goes. Drop closed businesses, duplicates and out-of-territory rows from the place list first, then enrich the survivors. With per-lead pricing — the $0.10 attaches only when a usable public contact is found — the waste line in this workflow is already small, but filtering first also keeps your client's list clean, which is the part they actually judge you on.

Refresh on the client's cadence, not on yours. Trades businesses open, close, move and rebrand constantly. A list delivered as a one-off spreadsheet is stale within a quarter. The agencies that retain clients sell the refresh: same territory, same trades, re-pulled monthly, with the diff highlighted — new businesses (your client's freshest prospects, highlighted at the top), closures removed, rating changes noted. A refresh costs a fraction of the original build because you only enrich the new and changed rows.

Packaging: how to sell a list without sounding like a list vendor

Clients do not want "data". They want a pipeline they can picture. The agencies we see succeed with this material package it in three forms:

  • The territory audit. Before any pitch: "There are 214 plumbing businesses in your service area. 61 have no website. 38 have under 10 reviews. Here are the counts for every trade you serve." The audit is a by-product of the place-extraction step — it costs a few dollars to produce — and it reframes the conversation from your price to their market.
  • The worked list. The enriched, filtered, segmented deliverable: one tab per trade, marketing signals attached, new businesses flagged. This is the list their team actually calls.
  • The monthly refresh. The retainer form: new entrants, closures, and movers, delivered on a schedule, priced as a service. This is the version that compounds — every refresh makes switching away from you more painful, because the client's pipeline history lives in your diffs.

Notice what is absent from the packaging: any claim about the data you cannot support. Do not promise emails for every record — in this vertical, four in ten records carrying an email is a realistic smoke-test figure, and a client told the truth renews, while a client promised 100% churns at the first empty column. Economics for the agency

Run the numbers on a mid-size territory: six trades across a metro area might return 1,500 places. At $0.004 per place that is $6; if 40% of the filtered list yields a public email and most yield a phone, the lead charges land in the tens of dollars. Your all-in data cost for a client-ready territory list is typically less than the cost of one hour of the salesperson who will work it. That is the pitch internally, too: the list is not a product you resell at a markup so much as the raw material that makes your retainer deliverable obviously worth its fee. Per-result pricing matters here because agency demand is spiky — a new client onboarding means three territories this week and none next week — and a subscription database charges you for the quiet weeks too. The territory audit, worked end to end

The audit is the agency's best first deliverable, so here is its production in concrete terms. A client — say a marketing agency onboarding a field-service software company — wants the picture for one metro territory across the six pack trades. You run the pack's searches locality by locality: perhaps forty searches in total for a mid-size metro. The raw extraction returns, illustratively, 2,300 place records; deduplication on name plus address brings it to about 1,900 distinct businesses; the status filter removes the permanently closed. At $0.004 per place, the entire enumeration costs under $10.

The audit itself is then arithmetic on fields you already hold: businesses per trade; the share with no website at all; the distribution of review counts (how many under 10 reviews, how many over 100); the share with a public email (expect the neighbourhood of our smoke-test figure — 40% of places, with contacts of some kind on 87%). Presented well, this one page repositions the engagement: the client stops negotiating your fee and starts discussing which segment to attack first. The enriched worked list follows for the segments they choose — you enrich those trades' records first, at $0.10 only where a usable public contact is found, and the remaining trades stay as a place-level map until the campaign expands. Notice the cost discipline this sequencing creates: the client never pays enrichment prices for segments they have not yet decided to work, and you never deliver a column of data nobody uses.

What the salesperson does with each field on Monday morning

A list earns its renewal in the first week of calling, so it is worth tracing the record into the rep's hands. Phone is the primary channel in this vertical — it is on nearly every record and it reaches the owner, because in a ten-person trades business the person answering the listed number is rarely more than one step from the decision. Rating and reviews are the opener: "You've got 212 reviews at 4.8 — this call is about protecting that" and "You've got 6 reviews — this call is about why the shop across town has 200" are two different, honest conversations, and the rep knows which one they are having before dialling. Website presence routes the account: no website means a build offer leads; a dated website means a redesign conversation; a good website with thin reviews means reputation is the wedge. Email, present on roughly four in ten records, runs the nurture sequence for accounts the phone did not reach. Status and freshness protect the rep's morale and the client's trust: nobody's first call of the day should be to a business that closed last spring. A list that supports all five of those uses is not a spreadsheet; it is the campaign, pre-built — and that is how it should be priced and presented.

The three list failures clients actually fire you for

Agencies rarely lose a client over a list's size. They lose them over three specific failures, all preventable at build time. Dead businesses. The client's rep calls a firm that shut down a year ago and concludes the whole list is archaeology. Live extraction plus the status filter eliminates this class entirely — it is the single strongest argument for building lists at order time rather than reselling stored inventory. Duplicates with different spellings. Two reps call the same roofer twice in a week because the list carried him as both "A1 Plumbing" and "A-1 Plumbing & Heating". Deduplication on name plus address — and a human glance at near-matches within the same street — is cheap insurance. Scope drift. The list quietly includes businesses outside the agreed territory or trade — the neighbouring county, the adjacent category — and the client's first spot-check finds one, and the spot-check is what they remember. Filtering to the agreed searches and showing the client the exact search list you ran turns scope from a trust risk into a demonstration of rigour. All three failures share a root: they are what happens when a list is a commodity bought by weight. A list built as a process — defined searches, live pull, filter, enrich, date-stamp — fails in none of these ways, and the process is visible to the client, which is precisely why it retains.

Seasonality: timing the build to the trades' calendar

Home services runs on seasons, and a list built with the calendar in mind outsells the same list built at random. HVAC demand spikes at the first heat wave and the first cold snap; roofing surges after storm seasons and into the dry months; plumbing peaks with winter freezes and the holiday hosting season; cleaning and landscaping crest in spring. The implication for agencies is twofold. First, build and refresh ahead of the client's selling season, not inside it: a territory list delivered six weeks before the season lets the client staff up, work the accounts and be the familiar name when demand hits; the same list delivered mid-surge arrives when the good businesses are too busy to take a sales call. Second, use the off-season signals in the data: in the quiet months, the businesses still accumulating reviews at pace are the ones investing through the trough — often the best accounts in the territory, and identifiable in a refresh diff precisely because their review counts moved while competitors' stood still. A monthly refresh cadence gives you twelve diffs a year; read against the trade calendar, those diffs tell you not just who is new and who closed, but who is pushing. That is the kind of observation that makes a client treat your report as intelligence rather than as a file delivery — and intelligence is the version of this product that renews itself.

From dataset to CRM: a field-mapping that survives contact with sales

The last mile of list quality is embarrassingly practical: the mapping between the dataset's fields and the client's CRM. Agencies that hand over raw exports watch the client's team mangle them in spreadsheets; agencies that deliver CRM-ready files watch their lists actually get worked. Map business name, trade category and address to the account object; phone, website and email to the contact channels; rating and review count to custom fields the client's reporting can see (these two fields drive segmentation and the audit narrative, so burying them in a notes field wastes the asset); status to a hard exclusion — closed businesses should be un-importable, not merely flagged; and source query plus extraction date to provenance fields on every record, so that when the refresh arrives, the diff can be computed inside the CRM rather than in your inbox. Add one field of your own: the segment (no-website, thin-reviews, established, new-since-last-refresh), computed at delivery time. The client's manager should be able to assign Monday's calls by filtering one column. None of this is technically difficult; all of it is the difference between a data delivery and a working system, and it is the part of the engagement the client cannot get from a database subscription at any price.

How agencies package the pricing (without selling "data")

Because your input cost is per result and known to the dollar, the deliverable can be packaged with unusual honesty. Three structures work in practice. Territory packages: a fixed-scope build — these suburbs, these trades, enriched and CRM-ready — priced as a project. Your data cost is a small, predictable fraction, and the client's scope questions ("add the east side?") have a concrete answer because the build is a defined set of searches. Refresh retainers: the monthly diff delivered as a standing service, priced on the cadence and territory size rather than on record counts the client cannot verify; your per-refresh data cost stays low because enrichment applies mainly to new and changed rows. Audit-first engagements: the territory audit delivered free or cheap as the diagnostic, with the worked list and refresh as the paid programme — the audit's counts come from the place-level pull, which costs single-digit dollars, so the economics of giving it away are sound. What all three avoid is reselling raw records by weight, which invites the client to comparison-shop your spreadsheet against a database's — a contest on the commodity layer. You are selling the process: defined territory, live pull, judgement applied, changes tracked. Price the process, and the data cost underneath it stays what it should be — a minor, known input.

Which trades does the pack cover?

What share of records will have an email?

In our smoke test, 87% of places carried contact details and 40% carried an email. Many sole traders publish a phone number and a contact form only. Build campaigns phone-first and treat email as a bonus channel in this vertical.

Can I define a custom service area?

Yes — run one search per locality in the service area and merge, deduplicating on name plus address. Practical territories ("these suburbs") map to a handful of searches each.

Can I white-label the deliverable for my client?
