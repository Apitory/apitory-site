Finding Real Estate Offices on Google Maps: A Practical Lead-Building Guide

TL;DR: Real estate B2B outreach should target offices — agencies, brokerages, property managers — not listings or individual agents. Google Maps is the only public dataset that enumerates offices with contacts attached, and a six-term Dallas–Fort Worth pack shows how the method works.

Why the Office, Not the Listing or the Agent, Is the Target

Every B2B campaign aimed at real estate starts with a unit-of-analysis mistake, and the mistake is the same in almost every case: the list is built from the wrong object. Listing sites show properties. Agent directories show individual agents. Neither is the buying unit. A brokerage's marketing budget is decided by the office; a property management company's software is bought by the company; a franchise's vendor relationships are negotiated at group level. The office is the unit that signs, pays and renews — and Google Maps is the only public dataset that enumerates offices directly, with their addresses, phones, websites, ratings and review counts, in one place.

Three Data Shapes, Only One Correct Buy

The distinction between the three data shapes is the whole purchase decision, so it is worth stating once more, precisely. Listings are properties: address, price, beds, photos. They are what buyers and renters search, and they are useless for B2B outreach — a listing's contact is the listing agent, whose authority extends to one property. Agent directories are people: name, photo, phone, sometimes an email. They are what a consumer uses to choose a representative, and they are the wrong unit for a vendor — an agent does not buy software for the brokerage. Offices are businesses: name, address, phone, website, rating, review count, and — once the website is visited — a published contact email. They are the unit that holds the budget, and they are what this Actor returns. The README states it plainly because the wrong buyer wastes the money and the right buyer saves it: if your campaign targets individual agents, this is the wrong tool; if it targets the businesses that employ them, this is the right one.

Two operational consequences follow. First, franchises and multi-office groups: a listing for a franchised location often links to the national brand's site, and the record's email may belong to head office rather than the branch. Group by website domain and phone number after extraction — the record carries both fields — and route outreach to the group's decision-making location, not to each branch in turn. Second, review counts as a prioritisation signal: an office with hundreds of reviews is an office with volume and revenue. Sort the extracted list by review count before working it, and the first calls go to the businesses most likely to be able to pay. The rating itself is a weaker signal than the count: a 5.0★ office with three reviews is a new office, not a beloved one. Volume first, rating second.

Why Property Managers Deserve Their Own Campaign

Property management companies deserve a paragraph of their own, because they are the segment where the data is most underused. A management company is a recurring-revenue business with dozens or hundreds of units under management — and it is a buyer of software, insurance, maintenance services, tenant screening, payment processing and marketing, all at once. It is also the segment most likely to be missed by a generic lead list, because its category on Maps is less uniform than "real estate agency": it appears under property management, apartment rental, real estate, and half a dozen neighbouring categories, and the search terms that catch it are the ones a buyer who only searches "real estate agency" never runs. The pack's three property-management terms exist for exactly that reason.

When working the list, segment the management companies out and treat them as their own campaign: the pitch is different (recurring operations, not transactions), the decision-maker is different (the operations manager, not the broker-owner), and the lifetime value is different (a management company that adopts your product keeps paying while its units stay under management). The same segmentation logic applies inside the agency half of the list: residential agencies, commercial brokers and apartment-rental agencies buy different things, and the category field in the record is the first cut — use it before you write a single email.

When a Clean Run Returns Redirect Links Instead of Websites

We hit this ourselves while building the engine. Our first smoke run of this Actor succeeded on paper — 15 records — but every record's website was a Google redirect URL (/url?q=...) instead of the office's real site, so the contact phase fetched nothing: 0 of 15 contacts and 0 emails, at a measured true cost of $0.2929. A fresh second run extracted clean direct URLs and found contacts for 14 of 15 offices (93.3%), which told us the redirect shape was an intermittent Maps panel variant, not a constant break.

We fixed it in the shared engine with a redirect-unwrap step, and the repair re-test (real estate agency in Dallas, cap 6) returned 6 records, 6 with websites, 0 redirect URLs, 5 contacts found and 2 with an email. That is the difference between a dataset that looks complete and one that is actually workable — the website field has to resolve before the contact field can mean anything.

Working the Office List: Calls, Timing and the Audit-First Pitch

A list is only worth what the outreach does with it, and real estate offices reward a different cadence than most verticals. Three practical adjustments matter.

Call first, and call the office number. Real estate is a phone-first industry: the listing carries the office's phone precisely because that is how the business runs, and the person who answers is often the office manager or the broker-owner — the decision-maker, not a gatekeeper. Email follows as the paper trail, with a one-line summary of the call and a link to whatever you promised.

Mind the week. Real estate offices work weekends — Saturday is a showing day, not a quiet day — and the quietest hours for a first contact are weekday mid-mornings, after the morning's showings are set and before the afternoon's appointments. Calling on a Saturday afternoon reaches an office in the middle of its busiest work; calling on a Tuesday morning reaches it at its most receptive.

Lead with the territory, not the product. An office that hears "we work with agencies in your metro" listens; an office that hears a feature list hangs up. The audit-first approach — "there are 214 agencies in your metro, 61 with no website, and here is where you stand" — works on real estate offices exactly as it works on home-services trades, because it opens with the office's own market rather than with your product. That is the same lesson as the data itself: a market is entered on its own terms, not translated.
